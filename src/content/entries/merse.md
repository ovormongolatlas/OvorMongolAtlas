Merse (1894–1934?), known in Chinese as Guo Daofu, was a Daur Mongol from Hulunbuir and one of the most influential nationalist organizers among the eastern Mongols [1, 2]. He helped found the Eastern Border People’s Party and the Inner Mongolian People’s Revolutionary Party, led part of the 1928 Barga uprising in Hulunbuir, and then, after coming to terms with Zhang Xueliang, founded a teachers’ college at Mukden that trained a generation of Mongol nationalists, [Khafungga](/entry/khafungga) among them. After the Japanese took Mukden in 1931 he fled to Hulunbuir, and in December he walked into the Soviet consulate at Manchuria Station, where the Soviet secret police detained him. The last record of his case is from 1934; what became of him is unknown.

*A note on sources.* The fullest account is Christopher Atwood’s archive-based *Young Mongols and Vigilantes* [1], which includes a year-by-year biography of Merse drawn from Mongolian, Chinese and Russian sources. [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid)’s memoir [2] tells parts of the story differently, and Nakami Tatsuo’s chapter [3] reconstructs his political thought from his own writings. Owen Lattimore, who knew him and drew on what Merse told him about Daur history, sketched him in *The Mongols of Manchuria* (1934), written while his fate was still unknown [5]. Where they disagree, the entry says so. The years above run from his birth to the last record of him; his death is not established.

## Early life (1894–1924)

Merse was born in 1894 in Mekhertü Ail in Hulunbuir, of the Manna lineage of the Gobol clan. His childhood name was Guo Junhuang and his Chinese name Guo Moxi, styled Daofu. His father, Minggu, was a lieutenant-commandant of two banners of the Solon Left Wing [1].

He studied at the Eight Banners School and, from 1910 to 1914, at Qiqihar’s No. 1 High School, graduating first in his class. In 1915 he entered the Russian Language Institute attached to the Foreign Ministry in Beijing. Early in 1917 he was baptized at Beijing’s Chinese Christian Church, and that year family troubles brought him back to Hulunbuir [1]. Jagchid instead has him studying at the Beijing College of Law and Politics, where he developed radical nationalist views [2].

Lattimore, who met him, describes a Hailar Daur of aristocratic family, of “extraordinary intellectual brilliance and immense personal courage,” who spoke Daur, Mongol, Manchu, Russian and Chinese [5].

With Fumingtai he founded the Hulunbuir Students Association in the winter of 1917 and the Hulunbuir School in 1918. He took part in the pan-Mongolist movement at Dauriia Station in 1919, opened a girls’ school in Mekhertü Ail in 1922, devised a new script, and in February 1923 completed his first book, *Menggu wenti* (“The Mongolian Question”). In the summer of 1922 the Heilongjiang provincial government ordered his arrest and he fled to Beijing. In November 1924 he addressed the First Great Khural of the Mongolian People’s Republic [1]. He married two Daur women, Xujie and Soujie, probably sisters, and had several daughters [6].

## Party leader (1924–1928)

Merse co-founded the Eastern Border People’s Party and was elected to the provisional committee of the [Inner Mongolian People’s Revolutionary Party](/entry/inner-mongolian-peoples-revolutionary-party) at its preparatory conference. At the party’s First Congress in October 1925 he became secretary of its Central Committee. In 1926–27 he worked for the party in the Yekhe Juu League, in Baotou and in Alashan; after a violent quarrel with Bai Yunti he was sent to Ulaanbaatar, and the Special Congress of August 1927 removed him from the party leadership. He returned as acting chairman that November [1]. Jagchid’s shorter account has Merse founding a Mongolian Youth Party with young eastern Inner Mongolians before joining the party under Bai Yunti, and being sent back to Hulunbuir after Bai Yunti was pushed out [2].

### His political thought

Nakami reconstructs part of Merse’s thinking from his Chinese-language writings of the mid-1920s [3]. Merse used the Mongol word *arad*, from the Russian *narod* (“the people,” which in Russian usage carried class meaning), but read it as “the nation.” On that basis he treated the Soviet-sponsored “People’s Revolution” in Outer Mongolia as, in substance, nationalism more than socialism. His own party’s name had the same double meaning. In Mongol it was the Dotaghadu Mongghol-un arad-un qubisqal-un nam, the “Inner Mongolian People’s Revolutionary Party”; in Chinese it was Neimenggu guomin gemingdang, deliberately echoing the Guomindang. Nakami reads that doubleness as a sign of the unsettled relationship between China and Mongolia at the time [3].

Merse also warned early that Japanese “pan-Asianism” hid designs on Mongolia, pointing to the Japanese dealings of [Babujab](/entry/babujab) and of Grigorii Semenov [3]. The [Japanese pan-Mongol strategy](/entry/japanese-pan-mongol-strategy) entry sets his diagnosis beside the Semenov material, which also appears in the [Chinggis Khan worship / imagery](/entry/chinggis-khan-worship-imagery) entry. It also records a 1935 episode, reported by Lattimore, in which invoking Merse’s name was enough to turn a group of Mongols in Japanese service from formal pro-Japanese answers to a franker statement of their loyalties [4].

## The 1928 Barga uprising

In August and September 1928 Merse commanded the eastern unit in the Hulunbuir uprising that Atwood calls the “Bargu Revolution” (Bargu is Atwood’s spelling of Barga), and held the seal of the Hulunbuir People’s Party. He and Fumingtai operated separately and seem not to have agreed on the plan; the insurgents proclaimed Fat Olzei their commander-in-chief. In September Merse made peace with the Manchurian authorities and went to Mukden to see Zhang Xueliang, who appointed him his secretary, a post Merse never actually filled. That autumn the Mongolian trade unions and the Seventh Congress of the Mongolian People’s Revolutionary Party denounced him, and later manifestos of his own party called him a renegade [1].

Jagchid tells this differently. He places a Hulunbuir independence movement led by Merse and Fumingtai in the winter of 1929, taking advantage of a Soviet military incursion into northern Manchuria, and has Merse accept Zhang Xueliang’s offer of a school when the Soviet troops withdrew the following spring [2]. Nakami dates Merse’s accommodation with Zhang Xueliang to September 1929 [3]. Atwood’s encyclopedia adds that the promised help from Mongolia and the Comintern never came, and that Merse turned strongly against the Soviet Union and against Mongolia, which he now saw as a puppet state [6].

## The Normal School at Mukden (1929–1931)

By the summer of 1929, Atwood writes, Merse had given up on autonomy for Hulunbuir. Back in Mukden, he took over a project already announced by the Mongolian Cultural Advancement Association and on 1 July 1929 opened the Northeast Mongolian Banners’ Normal School, with an attached primary school and a girls’ home-economics school. Merse was headmaster, and AltanOchir (Jin Henian), his opposite number in the talks that ended the uprising, was dean of instruction. Because such a school had been one of the conditions for ending the uprising, Zhang Xueliang himself chaired its board of trustees. Students, among them a sizeable minority of Han Chinese, came from the three eastern leagues and from the Butkha, Yekhe-Minggadai and Sürüg banners. Few came from Hulunbuir, because its new deputy lieutenant-general, Lingsheng, discouraged them [1].

Merse invited a wide range of speakers to the school, among them Sainbayar, the Panchen Lama, the young American journalist Owen Lattimore and [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub), an old enemy of the party [1]. Jagchid, who writes that the two men met through the Panchen Lama’s circle, describes both as sharing “the zeal and determination to struggle for Mongolian survival” [2]. Jagchid also records that Lingsheng came to treat Merse as an adviser and sent him to Nanjing as his representative to welcome the Panchen Lama [2]. Merse published two books in these years: *Menggu wenti jiangyanlun* (“Lectures on the Mongolian Question,” Mukden, November 1929, abridged in English in *Pacific Affairs* in August 1930) and *Hulun Buir wenti* (“The Hulun Buir Question,” Shanghai, November 1931) [1].

The school trained a generation of young Mongol intellectuals, several of whom went on to shape Inner Mongolia’s later politics, education and economy. Jagchid calls Khafungga “a former student of Merse” there [2], and Nakami independently names Khafungga among the disciples who carried Merse’s nationalism forward [3]. Merse “throughout his life exerted himself to educate Mongol youth,” in Nakami’s words, and many of his students later worked with the Japanese [3].

After coming to terms with Zhang Xueliang, Nakami writes, Merse concluded that a fully independent Mongol nation-state was out of reach and moved toward what Nakami calls “ethno-nationalism under China,” cultural autonomy within a Chinese framework. An alliance with a more advanced power, Russia or Japan, risked the Mongols being “assimilated or annihilated,” so alliance with the “equally weak” Han Chinese was the safer course [3].

Early in 1931 Merse was dismissed as headmaster. Zhang Xueliang then approved him to head a Mongolian Banners’ office in Beiping, a plan the Japanese invasion of September 1931 cancelled [1]. Lattimore, writing in 1934, saw Merse’s place in Zhang’s service as that of a political hostage. In his account Merse was removed from the school when it became clear that he was teaching Chinese, English and other subjects to make his pupils better Mongols and future leaders, and a Chinese principal was put in to make the school an instrument for turning Mongols into Chinese. Merse was then made Mongol secretary to Zhang and went on criticising Chinese policy as boldly as before [5]. Atwood instead has the secretaryship offered in 1928 and never actually taken up [1].

## Disappearance (1931)

In September 1931 Merse telegraphed Zhang Xueliang that he would rather die than live under Japanese rule, and retreated with four students to Nantun, south of Hailar. There he tried to organize resistance through the school’s network and old party members, with help from Su Bingwen, the military governor of Hulunbuir. One night in December he went to Manchuria Station, told a student he was going to the Soviet consulate and did not know when he would be back, and went in. On 11 December 1931 the OGPU, the Soviet secret police, detained him there on charges connected with the 1928 uprising. His case was handled in secret, and the last known document in it is dated 27 March 1934. Nothing is known of his sentence or his death [1].

The other sources differ:

- **Jagchid** says Merse went to Ulaanbaatar and “disappeared,” and records a rumor that Su Bingwen had him killed, adding that “there is no evidence to support this” [2]. In Atwood’s account Su Bingwen helped him.
- **Lattimore**, in 1934, writes that Merse slipped away from surveillance in Beiping after the Japanese invasion, reached Hailar and disappeared, and that Mongols believed Su Bingwen, the Chinese commander at Hailar, had seized and murdered him; he allowed that Merse might have escaped to Outer Mongolia or Siberia [5]. Nakami reports Lattimore as saying that a subordinate of Zhang Xueliang had Merse secretly assassinated, fearing he “might bring some Mongols over to the Japanese side” [4]. Su Bingwen was such a subordinate, but the motive does not appear in *The Mongols of Manchuria* and may come from another of Lattimore’s writings.
- **Nakami** rejects Lattimore’s account as “not accurate” and writes that Merse “was in fact taken under guard from the Soviet consulate in Manchouli to the Soviet Union” [3], which agrees with Atwood’s account.

## Open questions

- **His death.** He was detained by the OGPU in December 1931, and the last record of his case is from March 1934 [1].
- **Dates of the uprising and the settlement.** Launched in July 1928 (Atwood’s encyclopedia [6]) or fought in August–September 1928, with peace made that September (Atwood [1]); a Hulunbuir movement in the winter of 1929 and a settlement the following spring (Jagchid [2]); an accommodation with Zhang Xueliang in September 1929 (Nakami [3]).
- **The secretaryship.** Offered in 1928 and never filled (Atwood [1]), or held after his removal from the school (Lattimore [5]).
- **His schooling.** The Russian Language Institute of the Foreign Ministry (Atwood [1]) or the Beijing College of Law and Politics (Jagchid [2]).
