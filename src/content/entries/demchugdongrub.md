Demchugdongrub (known in English as Prince De, or Te Wang) was the ruling prince of the Sünid Right Banner in Inner Mongolia, a descendant of Chinggis Khan born in 1902. In the early 1930s he became the leading figure of the Inner Mongolian autonomy movement, and from 1936 he headed a series of Japanese-sponsored Mongol governments, ending with the Mongolian Autonomous State at Kalgan in 1941. After the war he tried to hold out in the west, and in 1949 he led a last government at Alashan. According to the accounts followed here he was arrested in Mongolia, extradited to Beijing in 1950, held as a war criminal, amnestied in 1963, and died in Hohhot in 1966.

*A note on sources.* The life rests mainly on [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid)’s biography, written by a man personally close to him, and on articles by the Mongolian researcher D. Zorigt, who draws on Mongolian archives. Western visitors, American press reports and John Hunter Boyle’s 1972 study add detail for the 1930s. For 1945 to 1952 there is also a run of CIA field reports that contradict the Jagchid and Zorigt account on whether and when he was captured. Where the sources disagree, the entry says so and leaves the question open.

## Birth and family (1902–1929)

His name is spelled “Demchugdongrob” in Jagchid’s biography and “Demchigdonrov” in Zorigt’s Mongolian-language articles [1, 2]. Jagchid counts him a thirty-first-generation descendant of Chinggis Khan through the Sünid line of the Golden Clan [1]. Boyle says thirtieth [3, p. 125], and the atlas records the difference without choosing.

The sources also differ on his birthplace. Jagchid places it in the Sünid Right Banner itself. Zorigt says the Shuluun Tsagaan Banner of the Chahar League, his mother’s home banner, and gives a reason: his elderly father Namjilwangchug, alarmed that his own birth year and his newborn son’s shared the tiger sign, reportedly kept mother and child away from him out of superstition, and died in 1902 without meeting his heir [2]. Jagchid explains why the birth date is uncertain. By the lunar calendar Demchugdongrub was born on the day, month and year of the tiger, an auspicious “four-tiger” birth, but in the morning with the hour unrecorded, following a Mongol custom of not divulging a birth time lest it invite a curse. His birthday was never officially celebrated, and Jagchid puts the Western-calendar date at the 12th or 17th of February 1902 [1].

His father’s death left the succession open for years. The senior assistant noble (tusalagchi) Pandei held the banner’s official seal and ran the banner for him. The Qing court confirmed the succession only in 1908, when the boy was about six, investing him as jasag toroyin junwang (ruling prince, second rank) of the [Sünid Right Banner](/entry/west-sunid-ruling-house). Jagchid says the six-year delay came from Beijing officials who stalled the paperwork until bribed [1, 2]. Because Mongol custom treated the full personal name of a person of rank as impolite, he was addressed as De noyan or De wangye, which gave him the English name “Prince De” [1]. After the fall of the Qing, Yuan Shikai’s Republican government raised many hereditary titles, and the young prince was promoted on paper to first-ranking prince, Khoshoi Dugureng Chin Wang, a distinction warmly received in the conservative Sünid Right Banner [1].

Pandei administered the banner until 1919, when Demchugdongrub reached his majority at eighteen nasun and took personal rule. (Nasun is age as Mongols traditionally counted it: a child is one nasun at birth and gains a year at each lunar New Year, so eighteen nasun in 1919 was about seventeen years of age by Western reckoning.) In 1924, at twenty-three nasun, he became deputy head of the Shilingol League [1, 2].

In 1929 the Swedish explorer Sven Hedin passed through the region and recorded the household. Hedin never uses a personal name. He writes only of “Sunit Wang,” the ruler of “Barun Sunit” (West Sünid), but the title and banner match Demchugdongrub’s, as Jagchid notes. Hedin places the prince’s headquarters at a Chinese-style palace with a private temple called Wangin Ortu, “the King’s Camp.” The Panchen (Tashi) Lama stayed there for about a month in July and August 1929 on his tour of Inner Mongolia, with some 10,000 pilgrims camped around it. A gathering that June at another monastery drew about 30,000 Mongols, of whom roughly 2,000 came from Barun Sünid. When Hedin’s own party reached the palace in November 1929, the prince was away, accompanying the Panchen Lama toward Mukden. Hedin put the banner’s army at about a thousand men and noted that the household had three motor cars [4].

The expedition had met him earlier, and filmed him. The documentary *Mit Sven Hedin durch Asiens Wüsten* (Swedish *Med Sven Hedin i Österled*, 1928), shot in 1927–28 by the expedition’s cameraman Paul Lieberenz, has a sequence of the prince, captioned “Sunit Wang,” hosting a Mongolian wrestling exhibition at his residence. Guests arrive by car and greet him at a table set with tea, wrestlers perform the eagle dance before their bouts, and he and his guests watch while smoking long-stemmed pipes [15]. The atlas maintainer identified him in this footage. It is likely among the earliest film of an Inner Mongolian prince recorded with the subject’s consent, and possibly the first, but the atlas has found no source that establishes it as the first, so that is recorded as the maintainer’s assessment. The atlas knows the sequence from an excerpt posted on YouTube, where it is dated 1928. Hedin does not appear in it, so it does not show whether he was present; where the original film is held is still to be recorded. As the [West Sünid ruling house](/entry/west-sunid-ruling-house) entry cautions, footage of the household should not be used alone to name the other people in it.

## The autonomy movement (1929–1935)

Through the late 1920s he built an independent base inside his own banner: schools, a clinic, a small textile mill, a workshop making small arms and ammunition, and a personal guard that passed a thousand men by 1926. He also sought outside patrons. He met Zhang Xueliang in Mukden in 1929 to obtain weapons, and Chiang Kai-shek in Wuhan in 1932 to discuss a Mongol cavalry division. In the winter of 1932 he courted the Mongol student community in Beijing, laying the ground for the movement’s youth support [2].

By the early 1930s he was the figure willing to press for more self-rule against both Chinese provincial encroachment and Japanese expansion. Jagchid, who was personally close to him, calls 1933 Prince De’s “golden age.” Working through other Mongol nobles, above all Prince Sodnamrabdan of Üjümüchin and Prince [Yondonwangchug](/entry/yondonwangchug), head of the [Ulanchab League](/entry/ulanchab-league), whom he persuaded in secret talks at Bat-khaalag, he gathered enough elite support to demand a “higher degree of autonomy” for Inner Mongolia from the Nanjing government [1].

Zorigt dates the first formal self-rule meeting at Bat-khaalag to July 26, 1933, followed the next day by a telegram to Nanjing demanding autonomy. A larger second meeting opened on October 9, 1933, a date on which Jagchid and Zorigt agree. Nanjing sent the envoys Huang Shaohong and Zhao Piyan. After months of dispute over whether the new body would be a full “autonomous government” or a lesser “local self-rule committee,” the compromise [Mongolian Political Affairs Council](/entry/mongolian-political-affairs-council) was inaugurated at Bat-khaalag on April 23, 1934, with Yondonwangchug as nominal chairman. Demchugdongrub held only the title of secretariat chief, but with the other senior princes elderly and largely inactive, both sources say real power rested with him [1, 2]. Jagchid presents the movement as Mongol-driven from the start. He quotes a purported memoir of Demchugdongrub in which a Japanese agent complains that the Mongols launched their autonomy push before Japanese forces were ready to back it [1].

American press coverage adds detail and one discrepancy. The *New York Times* reported the council’s inauguration with 28 members from leagues as far away as Kokonor, funding from Nanjing of about $50,000 a month, and a first priority of building a unified Mongolian army, a goal not realized in institutional form until the Mongolian Army General Headquarters of February 1936. It called Demchugdongrub vice chairman of the Shilingol delegation, not secretariat chief, and quoted him saying that Japanese agents had pressed the princes in March 1934 to seek annexation to Manchukuo and had been rebuffed [5]. The differing title is recorded here and left unreconciled, since foreign press coverage of internal Mongol titles was not always precise.

### Visitors in 1935

In 1935 observers began to write about him. The American Sinologist Owen Lattimore, writing after direct contact with Mongol autonomy activists, named Demchugdongrub, “of West Sunid, in Silingol League,” as the movement’s best-known leader. He dismissed rumors that the prince wanted to become a second Chinggis Khan and likened him to “an Inner Mongolian Roosevelt” steering between the movement’s radical and cautious wings toward pragmatic autonomy [6]. Lattimore tied the movement’s timing to the 1932 founding of Manchukuo; the [Mongolian Political Affairs Council](/entry/mongolian-political-affairs-council) entry gives that argument in full.

The Welsh journalist Gareth Jones visited the palace and, in a letter of July 14, 1935, described being a guest at a large Mongol feast honoring the mountain spirit. He saw a large man in dark blue silk, a black skull-cap with a red button, and a long queue. He guessed the prince’s age at 45 to 50, when Demchugdongrub was in fact 33. In an interview recorded in the letter, the prince told Jones he aimed to build an independent Mongol empire uniting the Mongols of Inner Mongolia with those of Soviet-aligned Outer Mongolia and those under Manchukuo [7]. Jones was a Western outsider whose interview passed from Mongolian to Chinese to German to English, so the exact wording attributed to the prince is approximate, and the letter has the condescension typical of the era’s travel writing. His physical observations and the substance of the interview are the reliable core, and his age guess and similar asides are treated more cautiously.

Western travellers’ accounts of [Hohhot](/entry/hohhot), collected by Isabelle Charleux, add incidental confirmation of his movements. Charleux, drawing on the Danish ethnographer Henning Haslund-Christensen, who had lived among Mongols and travelled with Hedin’s expeditions, notes that the prince, described there as the “country’s new master,” sometimes stayed at a Mongol encampment pitched outside the city in the mid-1930s [8]. Haslund-Christensen is a good eyewitness and a weak authority on the past: touring a Hohhot temple with the prince, he credited its foundation to Altan Khan, which Charleux shows to be mistaken [8]. His direct observations are treated here as more trustworthy than his claims about earlier history. Charleux also cites the American writer Graham Peck on Mongol princes’ presence in the city in general [8].

## Japan courts him (1933–1936)

Japanese interest in him began before [Mengjiang](/entry/mengjiang). The Kwantung Army drafted a policy for “guiding the Mongols” in July 1933, and the Zenrin Kyokai (“Friendly Neighbor Society”) began organizing among Inner Mongolians from January 1934. Doihara Kenji, the Kwantung Army intelligence chief later convicted as a Class A war criminal, met him in November 1934, and Itagaki Seishiro, another future convict, met him and Prince Sodnamrabdan in September 1935 to discuss Kwantung Army backing for a Mongol state [9].

Boyle dates a meeting between Demchugdongrub and Major General Doihara, then chief of the Mukden Special Service Agency (Tokumu-bu), to October 1935. By Boyle’s account Doihara convinced him that Mongol autonomy and Kwantung Army aims did not conflict, and by May 1936 plans for a Japanese-sponsored Mongol government were under way [3, p. 126]. The atlas records both dates, November 1934 and October 1935, because it cannot tell whether one meeting is misdated in one source or two meetings fell about a year apart. Boyle also relays, with a footnote citing Kawabe (p. 407), a report that the prince went to Hsinking in 1935, professed “total allegiance” to Manchukuo, and possibly received a large sum of money. Kawabe himself was “not certain” of it. The atlas tags this LOW-CONFIDENCE [3, p. 126 n.].

Business courted him as well. In 1935 Nagabuchi Saburō of the Manchuria Aviation Company went to Pailingmiao to urge him to expand the company’s operations and buy aircraft, reportedly telling him that “in this age of the airplane, the future lies in transportation across the sky, so the country with the most airplanes will master the globe.” The appeal played on the Chinggisid nomad-conqueror imagery he cultivated, and the company’s magazine recorded him as “exceptionally pleased” [10, citing *ManAir*, 1936]. A photograph from about this time shows him with [Li Shouxin](/entry/li-shouxin) and the Japanese officer Tanaka Ryūkichi. It is held via Wikimedia Commons, and its licensing has not been checked for use in this atlas.

In December 1935 he flew to the Manchukuo capital at Changchun, met the Kwantung Army commander Minami Jiro, and paid respects to the emperor Puyi, who on Japanese advice gave him an honorific princely title [9]. Around December 22 the *New York Times* correspondent Hugh Byas reported that he had proclaimed the independence of western Inner Mongolia, summoning thirty-three chiefs and claiming a territory of about two million people [11]. The report came through the paper’s Tokyo bureau from Japanese press dispatches, which colors its framing, and no other source used here describes a formal independence proclamation at that moment. It is consistent with the ambitions he had told Jones about five months earlier.

## Heading the Mongol governments (1936–1945)

According to Zorigt, a “[Mongolian Army General Headquarters](/entry/inner-mongolian-army)” was inaugurated at his palace on February 12, 1936, using the Chinggis Khan calendar and a new blue banner in place of Republic of China symbols. A first Mongolian Congress at Üjümüchin on April 24, 1936 debated a full Mongol state spanning Inner and Outer Mongolia and Kokonor, the union he had described to Jones, and settled on the more modest [Mongolian Military Government](/entry/mongolian-military-government), established at Dehua on May 12, 1936 with Demchugdongrub at its head [9, 1]. A Mongol attempt to take Suiyuan from Chinese forces that November ended in defeat at Bat-khaalag itself.

After full-scale war between China and Japan began in July 1937, his forces took Guisui (Hohhot) that October. A second Mongolian Congress there on October 27, 1937 created the [Mongolian Allied League Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-allied-league-autonomous-government), with the elderly Yondonwangchug as nominal chairman and Demchugdongrub as vice chairman and de facto head of administration [9]. It was formally inaugurated in Hohhot on December 1, 1937, with Demchugdongrub heading the Department of Administration and the more Japanese-aligned Li Shouxin as military commander [1]. With the formation that November of the Japanese-run “[Joint Committee of Mongolian Territories](/entry/united-committee-of-mengjiang),” which joined this government with the puppet regimes in [Southern Chahar](/entry/south-chahar-autonomous-government) and [Northern Shanxi](/entry/north-shansi-autonomous-government), the name “Mengjiang” came into common use for the combined territory [1].

Yondonwangchug died in March 1938, and a Third Mongolian Congress elected Demchugdongrub chairman outright on July 1, 1938 [1]. Boyle describes the same succession in his own terms: the prince succeeded the aged “Yun Wang” as head of what Boyle calls the “Federated Autonomous Government of Mongolia” on Yun Wang’s death in 1938 [3, p. 128]. The atlas takes “Yun Wang” as a probable variant of Yondonwangchug and Boyle’s name as his term for the Mongolian Allied League Autonomous Government. Boyle uses neither house name, so the identification stays probable and unconfirmed.

In September 1939 that government gave way to the [Mongolian Allied Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-allied-autonomous-government), which Demchugdongrub headed until August 1941 [1]. Boyle says he resigned in April 1939 as Director-General of the “United Committee of Mengchiang,” in dissatisfaction with Japanese policy, and that the Japanese Supreme Adviser [Kanai Shoji](/entry/kanai-shoji) took over [3, p. 134]. The atlas cannot tell from Boyle alone whether his body is the “Joint Committee of Mongolian Territories” [1], the “Mengjiang Federation Committee” (Tuo), or something separate, and records it as a third name in that open question. In Jagchid’s account he kept the title of chairman in these years but lost real power to Kanai. The atlas’s master chronology corroborates and extends several of Boyle’s claims through Hirokawa (2024); the [Mongolian Military Government](/entry/mongolian-military-government), [Mongolian Allied League Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-allied-league-autonomous-government) and [United Committee of Mengjiang](/entry/united-committee-of-mengjiang) entries give the government-by-government detail.

During this time he made secret contact with Chiang Kai-shek’s wartime capital at Chungking. With Li Shouxin he explored defecting from Japanese-held territory before the plot was uncovered; the [Mongolian Allied Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-allied-autonomous-government) entry covers the episode and the 1939–40 channel through Gao Rong and Ma Hansan. Boyle reports a separate approach in late 1939, through Chiang’s intelligence chief Tai Li, in which the prince offered to defect and Chiang told him to stay in ostensible collaboration with the Japanese, “neither recalcitrant nor subservient” (*pu k'ang pu pei*) [3, p. 134, citing Hangin, p. 40]. This rests on Boyle’s single informant, John Gombojab Hangin, as with the wordplay material in the [Jabsar](/entry/jabsar) entry, and is tagged SINGLE-SOURCE. Whether it is the same channel as the Gao Rong and Ma Hansan contact, an earlier or later stage of it, or a separate approach is not established, and the atlas does not merge them. The Chungking contacts complicate any picture of him as a simple Japanese puppet.

The last of these governments, the [Mongolian Autonomous State](/entry/mongolian-autonomous-state) (Mongolian: Monggol-un Obesuben Jasakhu Ulus), was inaugurated at [Kalgan](/entry/kalgan) on August 4, 1941, with Demchugdongrub as chairman. Jagchid calls it his “second golden era,” though one that “came too late.” Financial, police and transport power in Mengjiang stayed largely in Japanese hands throughout, exercised through a rotating series of Japanese “supreme advisors.” He spent much of the period maneuvering against Japanese officials, Kanai above all, who wanted to keep the Mongol government subordinate [1].

### Friends’ recollections

In a filmed interview with Caroline Humphrey on 21 May 1983, [Owen Lattimore](/entry/owen-lattimore) described him as a friend he held in high regard. Lattimore recalled that Demchugdongrub wanted autonomy, including control of taxation and education, but neither separation from China nor Japanese control. He said the prince knowingly kept young concealed communists on his staff and did not hand them over to the Japanese so long as they worked for Mongolian independence, and that he founded schools which made more young Inner Mongols literate than ever before. Neither the Chinese Nationalists nor the communists backed him, Lattimore said, while conservative princes who disliked his modernizing were likelier to deal with Chinese provincial governors or the Japanese. This is a friend’s recollection from nearly fifty years later, and the claim about protecting communists appears in no other source used here [12]. The [Owen Lattimore](/entry/owen-lattimore) entry explains how the interview is handled.

## After 1945

### Regrouping in the west (1945–1949)

After Japan’s defeat in August 1945 he tried to keep leading resistance from the west, sending his family into Soviet-Mongolian custody while he tried to rally scattered Mongol forces. In 1949 he led a final government, the [Mongolian Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-autonomous-government-alashan), based in Alashan [1, 13].

The postwar story comes from two sets of sources that agree on the outline and contradict each other on the central point. One is Jagchid and Zorigt, who say he was captured and imprisoned. The other is a run of CIA field reports of the late 1940s and early 1950s, which show him free and leading anti-Communist Mongol resistance in the Ningxia–Suiyuan–Alashan borderlands for two years longer. The reports are granular and contemporaneous, but they are unevaluated informant reporting, often relayed through Nationalist-aligned channels and anti-Communist in orientation, with troop figures that are partisan estimates rather than counts. One report calls the higher numbers “unduly high.” The atlas weighs them below its scholarly and memoir sources but finds them too specific and too numerous to set aside.

In June 1947 Fu Zuoyi telegraphed asking him to return to Inner Mongolia with promises of cooperation and help toward autonomy. He refused, replying that the proposal “had come one year too late” [16]. A March 1949 report has him, before leaving Beiping, turning down requests from the Soviet consulate and from Ulanhu’s eastern government to remain and cooperate. He reminded the Communist representatives that his family were already prisoners of the Soviets in the Mongolian People’s Republic and said that had he been willing to cooperate, he would have done so to free them [17]. This distrust complicates, without disproving, Zorigt’s account of a hopeful crossing into Mongolia in December 1949.

Through 1948–49 he sought Nationalist sanction to move west and lead the Northwest Mongols “on the same basis as Fu Zuoyi and the Ma brothers.” The government at first did not reply, possibly blocked by CC-Clique Mongols in the Mongolian-Tibetan Affairs Commission [18], and approval came later [19]. At Lanzhou in January and February 1949 he pressed for autonomy and weapons and got only a deputy’s verbal approval and, in the report’s phrase, “nothing concrete” [20]. His forces grew toward perhaps six thousand by mid-1949: about two thousand cavalry that had fought their way out of Manchuria, described as the only organized Nationalist force to escape, some fifteen hundred already in Suiyuan and Chahar, and possibly two thousand more under a commander named Hotoringga [21]. He failed to obtain Central Government funds and feared his unpaid cavalry would “resort to banditry” [22]. His representatives meanwhile kept contact in Shanghai and Guangzhou with the China Democratic League and the Kuomintang Revolutionary Committee, including Luo Longji, who pledged support and cooperation “in any coalition government” [23, 24]. Nationalist-aligned Hong Kong press reported material and psychological backing for his resistance and had his representatives meeting Wang Shijie in Taiwan in 1950, a channel with an obvious partisan slant [25].

### Captured, or still fighting? (1949–1952)

According to Zorigt, at the end of December 1949 he crossed into the Mongolian People’s Republic, reportedly believing Marshal Choibalsan wanted to discuss a joint push toward unifying Inner and Outer Mongolia. Instead he was held, interrogated for months by Mongolian and Soviet officials, and formally arrested on February 27, 1950 as an alleged enemy of the state [13]. Jagchid gives September 18, 1950 as the date he was extradited to Beijing [1]. Zorigt places it vaguely in “late 1950” [13].

The CIA reports cannot be squared with that dating. They place him free and active for two more years. In early 1950 he was raiding Ningxia from an Alashan base [26]. That February his representatives were negotiating with the local Communist authorities in Ningxia, which an analyst read as a man “in desperate circumstances” probably “seeking a local tactical advantage” [27]. By August 1950 he reportedly commanded about twenty thousand men after a defected Fu Zuoyi force joined him, and his representatives gave “a flat refusal” to a Communist negotiating mission [28]. In May 1950 the Chinese Communists sent [Bai Haifeng](/entry/bai-haifeng) and He Zhaolin to win him over, without success [29]. By mid-1951 his forces were reported at around ten thousand, with Fu Zuoyi cavalry defecting to him rather than deploy to Korea [30], and he fought a two-day engagement in Alashan before withdrawing to Ejine Torghut [31]. As late as June 1952 he was reported living as a nomad off his herds near the Suiyuan–Ningxia–Mongolian People’s Republic tripoint with former Ma Hongkui troops [32].

One December 1950 report partly bridges the accounts. It describes his forces as fragmented into small scattered units, with Demchugdongrub “in the desert area” and only a few followers [33]. That would let a rump resistance operate under his name while his own position collapsed. It does not reconcile first-hand sightings of him free and commanding troops in 1951–52 with an arrest in early 1950. The atlas records the conflict between Zorigt’s archival dating and the CIA reporting and does not resolve it. The troop numbers throughout are widely varying informant estimates.

The edited scholarly volume *Imperial Japan and National Identities in Asia, 1895–1945* gives what it presents as the orthodox account: that he fled to Outer Mongolia and was later jailed by the Chinese communist government, which made him confess that his wartime actions were “traitorous” [14]. The atlas holds only a pasted excerpt of the volume, so the chapter and page are not verified. It broadly follows Zorigt’s outline, and it is partly downstream of Jagchid for material of this kind. It is recorded as a further voice on Zorigt’s side of the conflict and not as a tiebreaker.

### Imprisonment and death (1950–1966)

In the account Jagchid and Zorigt follow, he was held in China as a war criminal and reportedly sent to labor at the Fushun coal mines. Jagchid says he was released under a general amnesty on April 9, 1963 after about twelve and a half years in custody. Accounts vary on what followed: living under surveillance, working in an institute library, or informal house arrest in Hohhot. Lattimore recalled in 1983 that he was sentenced to death, had it commuted to life, and later worked as librarian of a Mongol library he had created [12].

Jagchid says three different dates have been reported for his death, April 25, May 23 and June 4, 1966, and that he could not determine which is correct, though he leans toward May 23 as the one most often cited, placing the death in Hohhot at the start of the Cultural Revolution. Zorigt says only that he “died in 1966.” Jagchid reports that his ashes were later scattered on the mountain that had held the oboo (ceremonial cairn) of the Sünid Right Banner. He adds that the Cultural Revolution destroyed many of his accomplishments, and that [Khafungga](/entry/khafungga) and most of his other compatriots who had struggled for Mongolian nationalistic aspirations did not escape persecution either. Khafungga belonged to a very different, Communist-aligned strand of that struggle [1].

### His family

Zorigt says his wife and children were arrested in Mongolia the day after his own arrest and sent into internal exile within Mongolia [13]. Jagchid says that during his imprisonment the family, except his eldest son, whose fate Jagchid says was unknown to him, were sent from Mongolia back into China [1]. Both agree the household suffered serious hardship.

Jagchid names his wife Sebjidma, in the narrative and in the book’s index [1, p. 448]. Zorigt’s article on the family’s fate names her Renchintsoo, in the Mongolian original “Дэ вангийн хатан Рэнчинцоо” (“De Wang’s khatan Renchintsoo”) [13]. Khatan is a rank-word for a consort, and “Renchintsoo” is given as a personal name beside it, so the difference is not one source using a title where the other uses a name. Both names may be genuine, belonging to different wives or to one woman at different times, or one source may be mistaken. Hedin’s 1929 account shows plural marriage in the family: two dowager queens living in Sünid, one of them the prince’s mother, and a father who had had several wives. He describes the prince’s own wife at that time as having recently borne an heir after losing four earlier children, and as living in seclusion [4]. This does not decide which name belongs to which wife.

The CIA reports partly fill the gap Jagchid leaves on the eldest son. Three of them place a son of Demchugdongrub, garbled in the originals as “Te Hua” and also “Yugar-Jap,” in the Mongolian People’s Republic in 1945–47: captured with the family at the Japanese collapse and sent alone to a middle school in Ulaanbaatar [34], and still there in early 1947, with the prince unable to locate his family [35]. The [1945 provisional government](/entry/inner-mongolian-republican-provisional-government) entry notes the same capture. This supports the fact that the eldest son was held there. It does not give his name. Identifying him with Dugarsuren (also Dugursulong), named in later family sources, is the atlas’s own inference, and none of the reports asserts it.

## Open questions

- **Birth.** Birthplace (Sünid Right Banner or Shuluun Tsagaan Banner) and the exact date (February 12 or 17, 1902).
- **Descent.** Thirty-first generation (Jagchid [1]) or thirtieth (Boyle [3, p. 125]).
- **Doihara.** A meeting in November 1934 (Zorigt [9]) or October 1935 (Boyle [3, p. 126]).
- **Boyle’s “United Committee of Mengchiang.”** Whether it is the Joint Committee of Mongolian Territories (Jagchid [1]), the Mengjiang Federation Committee (Tuo), or a separate body.
- **The Tai Li contact of late 1939.** Whether it is the same channel as the Gao Rong and Ma Hansan contact.
- **Capture.** Arrested in Mongolia on February 27, 1950 (Zorigt [13]), or free and fighting until at least 1952 (CIA reports [16–35]).
- **Death.** April 25, May 23 or June 4, 1966 (Jagchid [1]).
- **His wife.** Sebjidma, Renchintsoo, or two women.

See also the [Suiyuan campaign (1936)](/entry/suiyuan-campaign-1936) and [Operation Chahar (1937)](/entry/chahar-operation-1937) entries. His later friends and the movement that replaced his are covered in the [Dilowa Khutugtu](/entry/dilowa-khutugtu) and [Ulanhu](/entry/ulanhu) entries.
