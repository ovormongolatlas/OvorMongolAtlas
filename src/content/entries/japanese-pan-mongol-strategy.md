From the early 1930s Japan courted the Mongols as a people who might be drawn away from China and, through Inner Mongolia, from the Soviet-dominated Mongolian People’s Republic. The courtship ran through army policy, a civilian society for Mongol welfare and propaganda, and religious schemes, and it always stopped short of the Mongol state the nationalists wanted. This entry gathers what the atlas’s sources say about it; the government entries, above all [Mengjiang](/entry/mengjiang) and the [Mongolian Autonomous State](/entry/mongolian-autonomous-state), follow the politics in detail.

## Merse’s early warning

Before the Mengjiang years the Daur nationalist [Merse](/entry/merse) warned in his own writings that Japanese “pan-Asianism” hid designs on Mongolia. As evidence he pointed to the Japanese dealings of Babujab, the Mongol commander of the 1910s, and of Grigorii Semenov, the White Russian ataman whose Japanese-backed pan-Mongol movement in Transbaikalia is treated in the [Chinggis Khan worship / imagery](/entry/chinggis-khan-worship-imagery) entry [1]. His warning came years before the Kwantung Army began to court [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub).

## Army policy and the Zenrin Kyōkai (1933–1934)

On 16 July 1933 the staff of the Kwantung Army issued an outline of policy for “guiding the Mongols.” In western Inner Mongolia, it said, peaceful cultural work, and economic means above all, should lead the Mongols to draw close to Japan and Manchukuo of their own accord and away from China, and an autonomous government should be set up quickly [2]. In January 1934 a society for work among the Mongols, the Zenrin Kyōkai (善隣協會, “Friendly Neighbours Association”), was founded in Tokyo. It ran a section for Mongol students, recruited students and built a hostel for them, and set up branches in Chahar, Shilingol, Doloon Nuur and several banners, with clinics for people and livestock, primary schools and medical teams [2].

The society’s first pamphlet, 『蒙古とはどんな處か』 (“What Kind of Place Is Mongolia?”, 1934), shows how it presented itself [3]. It opens by insisting that Mongolia must be measured with a Mongol yardstick, and argues that the land north of the Great Wall had always been the Mongols’ own, the graves of their ancestors, which they had held against the Chinese by force of arms; China had never ruled it except for brief moments. Its conclusion sets out the society’s purpose. Four million Mongols, it says, were threatened by Soviet power from the north and by Chinese assimilation from the south, and the awakening of the Mongol nation was the first step toward peace in East Asia and the spread of the Imperial Way; Japan, it insists, had no wish to seize other peoples’ land. The society called itself the one true movement to understand the Mongols and be their friend. Its programme in Japan was a Tokyo headquarters and an Osaka branch, publicity about Mongolia, guidance for Mongol students, a research institute and library; on the continent, offices in Hsinking and key places in Mongolia, clinics and travelling doctors, schooling for Mongol children, help for Mongol industry and trade, surveys of Mongol resources, and publicity about Japan [3].

## A new Jebtsundamba (1938–1940)

Japan also tried to use Mongol Buddhism. Paul Hyer, drawing on interviews with Japanese officers and officials and with the [Dilowa Khutugtu](/entry/dilowa-khutugtu), Sechin Jagchid and Gombojab Hangin, describes three schemes to install a ninth Jebtsundamba Khutugtu, the incarnate lama who had been Mongolia’s Bogd Khan, in Inner Mongolia [4]. A Japanese Foreign Ministry paper stated the premise: the lamas were the key to winning the Mongols, as the Qing had known. The aims were to win Mongol support for the occupation and to build a pan-Mongol movement that could carry Japanese influence north into Khalkha [4].

- **The Xing’an plan, 1938.** Colonel Yano Mitsuji had proposed a new Jebtsundamba soon after 1931. In 1938 Goshima Tokushirō of the cultural section of the Xing’an Bureau revived the plan, with Kwantung Army intelligence as sponsor and 20,000 yen from the South Manchuria Railway. The Tibetan Ngachen Rinpoche in Beijing agreed to help, and two young Japanese trained by army intelligence went to Tibet with his party in early 1939; one, Nomoto Jinzō, stayed about a year. Lhasa gave no approval [4].
- **The Kalgan plan, 1939.** Colonel Kanagawa Kōsaku, who later promoted the Chinggis Khan shrine at Wangyemiao, worked through a young lama from Labrang, Lang Tsang, who carried a letter from Demchugdongrub to the head of the Sakya sect asking for an oracle. He returned in the summer of 1939 with a vague oracle and a letter from the head lama of Labrang pointing to an eleven-year-old boy in Amdo. Demchugdongrub, displeased at the opportunism, ended the scheme [4]. Jagchid told Hyer that the prince thought such matters belonged to the old Manchu times, though he saw that a new Jebtsundamba could help unite the Mongols [4].
- **The Dilowa’s journey.** The Japanese then agreed to work through the Dilowa Khutugtu, who wanted to go to Tibet and saw a chance to escape Japanese control. When he reached Hong Kong the British put him on a plane to Chongqing, where he spent the war under Chinese detention [4]. The Dilowa entry follows his story.

Some Japanese intelligence men, Hyer adds, thought Demchugdongrub’s youngest son a suitable candidate; nothing came of it. Hyer concludes, with Jagchid, that Japan failed in its efforts to use Mongol religion [4].

## The limits of the courtship

Tokyo was never of one mind. The Foreign Ministry’s files hold one paper supporting Inner Mongolian independence and another opposing it, and once Japan needed Wang Jingwei’s collaborationist government in Nanjing it accepted Chinese sovereignty over the Mongol government (30 November 1939). The Mongolian Autonomous State of 1941 was a renaming won by Demchugdongrub, not the state he asked for [5]. The [Mongolian Autonomous State](/entry/mongolian-autonomous-state) entry follows that story.

## The 1935 Peking test

An episode reported by Owen Lattimore and relayed by Nakami shows how conditional the loyalty bought by this courtship could be. In 1935 Lattimore interviewed several Mongols in Japanese service in Peking and at first got the standard pro-Japanese answers. When he showed that he knew Mongol and was a friend of Merse, the senior Mongol present told him, in effect, not to look at their uniforms: when the day came they would act as Mongols and not as paid soldiers of the Japanese [1, 6]. Nakami reads this as evidence that such loyalty was instrumental and conditional, without claiming that every Mongol in Japanese uniform felt the same [1]. Merse’s name, invoked by an outsider, was what turned a formal interview into a frank one.
