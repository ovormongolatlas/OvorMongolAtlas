Gada Meiren (1893–1931; personal name Nadmid) was a *meiren*, a senior military officer of the Darhan Banner, the Khorchin Left Wing Middle Banner of the Jirim League in eastern Inner Mongolia [1]. In 1929 he led a revolt against a colonization project that was taking his banner’s land for Chinese settlers and evicting its Mongol farmers. The revolt was crushed and he was killed in 1931, but the song of his rising is still widely sung [1].

*A note on sources.* The entry rests on Christopher Atwood’s encyclopedia [1] and on Owen Lattimore’s *The Mongols of Manchuria* [2], written within three years of the events. Lattimore does not name the leader, but his place, time and story match. The fullest study, by Borjigin Burensain, is not yet held (see the open questions).

## The land and the rising (1929–1931)

In Lattimore’s account a great tract of the banner north of Tongliao was opened for colonization about 1930 and a new Chinese county, Liaobei, was set up on it. The authorities cited an old ruling that the Mongols’ title deeds were invalid, and the Mongols, almost all of them settled farmers, were evicted; few could buy their land back. The Mongols said the land companies were agents of followers of Zhang Xueliang, and that the Young Marshal himself had money in the scheme [2]. Atwood describes it as one of the warlord-era projects in which the creation of a county came before the settlers, and calls the rising that followed the last great revolt against Chinese colonization in Inner Mongolia [1].

The banner’s prince lived mostly in Mukden and Beiping and was related by marriage to Zhang Xueliang’s family, which left him, as Lattimore puts it, in an awkward position. The banner’s chief military officer went to Mukden and pleaded for the evicted Mongols before the local Kuomintang. He was imprisoned, later released, and then led a desperate rising [2]. Troops and artillery were brought against the insurgents, other Mongols dared not help them, and several thousand people, women and children among them, were driven from their villages westward with no provisions for the winter and no livestock to live on. The leader was killed in action, and the last of the “bandits” had not been cleared when the Japanese struck at Mukden on 18 September 1931 [2]. Atwood dates the revolt to 1929–1931 and notes that, although the Mongol farmers were evicted, the Japanese occupation forestalled the actual colonization [1].

## His death and Li Shouxin

The atlas’s master chronology places his death at the Shar Mörön river on about 4–5 April 1931, at the hands of a Fengtian force under [Li Shouxin](/entry/li-shouxin); this detail is reference-tier and not yet checked against a scholarly source. Narangerel’s study of Li Shouxin confirms the link from Li’s own side: in old age Li set down the truth of how he had suppressed the rising and shot Gada Meiren, and said that when he learned what the rising had really been about he “felt very regretful.” Narangerel suggests that years of humiliation by Nationalist warlords had made him rethink his rash youth [3].

## Memory

The song of Gada Meiren is the best-known memory of the rising [1]. A folk song and a narrative poem of 1979 both make the warlord Zhang Zuolin the villain, though he was killed in 1928 and the land sale came under his son Zhang Xueliang, and some popular accounts wrongly make the settlers Japanese. The [land-reclamation](/entry/land-reclamation) entry places the rising in the longer history of Chinese settlement on Mongol land.

## Open questions

- **The date of the rising.** 1929 (Atwood [1]) or a colonization opened in 1930 (Lattimore [2]); the two may describe the decision and its execution.
- **His death.** Shar Mörön, April 1931 (master chronology, reference-tier); Lattimore and Atwood say only that he was killed in 1931.
- **The fullest study.** Borjigin Burensain, “The Reclamation Issue of Liao-Bei-Huang and Gada Meirin’s Uprising,” *Waseda Journal of Literature Researches* 47, no. 4 (2002): 113–126, and the English version, chapter 4 of his *The Agricultural Mongols: Land Reclamation and the Formation of Mongolian Village Society in Modern China* (Shumpusha, 2017), are the next sources to get.
