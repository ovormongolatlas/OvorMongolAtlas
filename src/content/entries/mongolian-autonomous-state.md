The Mongolian Autonomous State (Mongolian *Monggol-un Öbesüben Jasaqu Ulus*; Japanese 蒙古自治邦, *Mōko jichihō*) was the last form of the [Mengjiang](/entry/mengjiang) regime. It was inaugurated at [Kalgan](/entry/kalgan) on 4 August 1941 with [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub) as chairman, and it ended with Japan’s surrender in August 1945. It replaced the [Mongolian Allied Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-allied-autonomous-government) of 1939, which the Japanese had formed by merging the Mongol government with two Chinese regimes. The new state was the result of Demchugdongrub’s pressure on Tokyo, and under it the Mongols recovered much of the administration of their own lands, though the Japanese kept finance, transport and the police in the Chinese districts [1, 2].

*A note on sources.* [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid), who served in its administration as secretary to Prince Sung, gives the inside account [1]. Ganbagana, a historian at Akita International University, uses Japanese Foreign Ministry and Army archives; he argues that earlier scholarship dwelt on the 1939 government, sometimes called a “second Manchukuo,” and passed over this state, which in his reading shows the Mongol side’s own aims [2]. Christopher Atwood’s encyclopedia gives a short outside summary [3].

## Origins (1937–1941)

After his forces took Hohhot in October 1937, Demchugdongrub wanted a Mongol government of its own. The Kwantung Army, represented by [Kanai Shoji](/entry/kanai-shoji), wanted the opposite: a single government joining the Mongols with the Chinese regimes of [South Chahar](/entry/south-chahar-autonomous-government) and [North Shansi](/entry/north-shansi-autonomous-government). In the autumn of 1938 the prince went to Tokyo and put two plans for a state to the army minister, Itagaki Seishirō, one for western Inner Mongolia alone and one that included the two Chinese regions as the Kwantung Army wished. Neither was adopted, and the merged government was formed in September 1939 [2]. Tokyo was itself divided. The Foreign Ministry’s archive holds one paper supporting Inner Mongolian independence and another, an outline of Mongol policy, opposing it [2].

The rise of Wang Jingwei’s collaborationist government in Nanjing made things worse for the Mongols. Wang opposed any separation of Inner Mongolia from China, and Japan gave way to win his support. In its agreement with Wang of 30 November 1939 Japan accepted Chinese sovereignty over the Mongol government, and the Qingdao conference of January 1940 confirmed that the Mongol government came under Wang’s [1, 2]. Demchugdongrub never accepted Wang’s government, and when Wang visited Kalgan in April 1940 the prince refused to receive him [2]. Mongol feeling toward Japan cooled. The Japanese writer Matsuzaki Yū recorded that the Mongols, who had once looked on the Japanese as brothers, now looked on them as enemies; the prince reopened secret contact with Chiang Kai-shek’s government, and a secret Mongol youth party working for independence spread among the young [2].

In February 1941 Demchugdongrub went to Japan a second time, carrying a proposal in his own hand, the “Draft for Hastening the Founding of a Mongol State” (蒙古建国促進案), for Prime Minister Konoe Fumimaro. It asked for a fully independent state or, failing that, a Mongolian Autonomous State. The Japanese were reluctant, since recognising a Mongol state would cause trouble with Wang Jingwei, and Konoe declined to see him, pleading illness. The Japanese consulate in Mengjiang warned the Foreign Ministry that Mongol goodwill would be lost if the prince was refused. Tokyo finally agreed to a change of name, on condition that the old name be kept in dealings with foreign governments [2].

The name was a compromise. To avoid offending Wang Jingwei, the Japanese avoided 国 (*guo*, country) and used 邦 (*bang*, state), but both translate into Mongolian as *ulus*, a word that satisfied Mongol feeling [1, 2]. Jagchid adds that the prince took the idea of autonomy from the self-governing dominions of the British Empire, as a form to follow if independence could not be had [1]. Jagchid calls it the start of his “second golden era,” one that “came too late” [1].

## The state (1941–1945)

The inauguration took place on the morning of 4 August 1941 in the courtyard of the government offices in Kalgan, arranged in haste because the prince and his planners feared the Japanese might change their minds [1]. Some Chinese intellectuals suspected it was a step toward separation from China, while most Mongols saw it as the dawn of Mongolian independence [2]. On 18 September Wang Yitang, chairman of the Japanese-sponsored North China Political Affairs Committee, paid a formal visit to Kalgan, which Jagchid reads as recognition of the prince as head of an autonomous state [1].

The Japanese then changed their advisers. In mid-October 1941 the chief of staff of the Japanese army in Mongolia, whom Jagchid names as Takahashi Shigekasu, a supporter of [Wu Heling](/entry/wu-heling), was transferred, and his successor, Major General Imamura Toyojirō, helped remove Kanai [1]. In November Kanai was pressed to resign, and at the prince’s request he was replaced as supreme adviser by Ōhashi Chūichi, a diplomat friendly to the Mongols. Ōhashi soon clashed with the Japanese army in Mongolia and was removed in the summer of 1942 [1, 2]. The prince and the Japanese army then agreed to abolish the post of supreme adviser: neither side wanted any man in the government to rank with its own chief. The minister of general affairs, Takeuchi Tetsuo, became the highest Japanese official, under the supervision of the chairman [1]. Atwood dates the prince’s recovery of more than nominal authority to Kanai’s transfer [3].

The Mongols now ran their own leagues and banners. Jagchid writes that Japanese advisers seldom interfered in Mongol administration [1, as cited in 2], though finance, the economy, transport and the police in the Chinese districts stayed in Japanese hands as before [1]. Ganbagana describes the governing principle as separate rule of Chinese and Mongols. The Chinese and Hui districts came under the Ministry of the Interior, and everything touching Mongol lands under the Mongolian Rehabilitation Committee (興蒙委員会), which Ganbagana calls in effect a smaller Mongol government [2]. The committee, under Prince Sungjingwangchug, had bureaus of general affairs, civil administration, education, industry and public order, with an institute for Mongol cultural research, a livestock testing station, a vaccination office and breeding centres. Its Japanese staff were relabelled advisers and assistants, which Jagchid says made them better behaved than Japanese elsewhere in the government. The league heads and leading jasags and ambans met twice a year in its conference, where they could speak but had no vote [1]. From the autumn of 1941 the government also ran a three-month training course for banner officials under forty [1]. Ganbagana sums up the committee’s work as economic construction, the spread of education and the raising of living standards, carried on for only four years before the war ended it [2].

## Reform, nationalism and the queue campaign

Jagchid portrays these years as ones in which Demchugdongrub pressed a nationalist reform agenda. In the summer of 1944 Jagchid and Budabala, the vice head of the Shilingol League, urged him to have Mongol men cut off their queues, arguing that the braid was not a style of Chinggis Khan’s era but a “shameful symbol” of subjugation imposed after the Manchu conquest. They proposed ordering the cut on 4 August, the state’s anniversary [1]. The appeal to a Chinggisid past against an imposed custom belongs with the other uses of Chinggis Khan’s memory traced in the [Chinggis Khan worship / imagery](/entry/chinggis-khan-worship-imagery) entry. Demchugdongrub agreed only conditionally: Shilingol could order it outright, Ulanchab only after he explained it in person, and he vowed to keep the queue on his own head until Mongolia truly won its independence [1]. Jagchid concedes the practical effect was small, since most of Inner Mongolia outside Shilingol, Ulanchab and part of Yekejuu had already given up the queue, but he treats the order as politically significant.

The episode also split the movement. When Rinchinsengge, the vice head of the Ulanchab League, cut his queue without authorisation, Demchugdongrub rebuked him and made him apologise in public. The insult to his father, the former league head Prince Babudorji, was exploited by the Chinese general Fu Zuoyi, who drew Babudorji’s family over to his headquarters, so that, in Jagchid’s words, “a split was created in the unification effort through the cutting off of a queue” [1]. Throughout the period Demchugdongrub and Wu Heling also worked to take control of the [Mongolian Army](/entry/inner-mongolian-army) back from the Japanese, setting up a Military Affairs Ministry for the purpose [1].

The state collapsed in August 1945, when the Soviet and Mongolian armies entered Inner Mongolia and Demchugdongrub left Kalgan for Beiping; the [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub) entry covers its last days.

## Open questions

- **Date of founding.** 4 August 1941 (Jagchid [1], Ganbagana [2]); the [Mongolian Allied Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-allied-autonomous-government) entry gives 1 August for the reorganisation.
- **The chief of staff transferred in 1941.** Takahashi Shigekasu (Jagchid [1]); Ganbagana’s Mongolian text appears to name Ōhashi Kumao, whom Jagchid describes as a senior Japanese political adviser.
