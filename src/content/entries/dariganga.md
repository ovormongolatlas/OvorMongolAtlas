Dariganga was a district of imperial herds on the southern edge of Khalkha, set apart under the Qing to raise horses, camels and sheep for the court in Beijing. Although Khalkha princes held the land all round it, it answered to the Chahar administration at Kalgan, and it had no hereditary nobility of its own. That position, inside Khalkha on the ground but tied to Inner Mongolia on paper, is why its status was disputed for half a century after the Qing fell. Today the district lies in Mongolia’s Sükhbaatar province, and the Dariganga Mongols are counted as their own subethnic group (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, “Dariganga,” p. 132).

The atlas has found no mention of Dariganga in [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid)’s *The Last Mongol Prince*: the name does not appear in the text or the index.

## Under the Qing

The sources disagree on when the herds were founded.

- **1691.** Chinese Wikipedia says the Setsen Khan and Tüsheet Khan aimags presented Dariganga to the Qing court at the Dolonnor assembly of 1691 (Kangxi 30), when the Khalkha princes submitted to the Qing (Chinese Wikipedia, “達里岡厓牧場”).
- **1696.** Isabelle Charleux, citing a 2017 conference paper by B. Tamirjav, dates the establishment of the pastures to 1696 (Charleux).
- **1697.** Christopher Atwood says the Kangxi emperor set up a special imperial stud in the Dariganga area in 1697, after driving the Zünghars out of Khalkha (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 132).

These need not exclude one another: a grant of the land in 1691 and the founding of the herds a few years later would fit all three. The atlas has not seen a source that says so, however, and records all three dates.

According to Atwood, the stud was manned by Khalkhas from Setsen Khan aimag and by Mongols of the Abaga and Sünid banners of Shilingol. Over time it grew into two camel herds, two horse herds and a sheep herd, sending 800 stallions, 300 bull camels and first 4,000, then (after 1831) 7,270 rams to the court each year (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 132). The twelve Chahar banners and pastures and the Dariganga pastures together came under the Manchu military governor (dutong) at Kalgan (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, “Chakhar,” p. 88). Atwood’s later work names the office directly: the amban of the “Three Banners and Dariganga Sheep and Cattle Herds” (San qi Daligangga niu yang qun zongguan), whose charge was three separate blocks of land in the Chahar banners plus Dariganga itself (Atwood, *Young Mongols and Vigilantes*, p. 510 and n. 16). Chinese Wikipedia instead places the pasture under the Imperial Household Department’s stables office (上駟院), managed by the Shangdu superintendent (商都總管). The two accounts give different offices and are recorded side by side.

The district had its own sacred mountains. Charleux, again citing Tamirjav, relates that the herds’ first leaders built their headquarters in the Yeke Bulag valley and ordered a complex of thirteen stone ovoos on Dashlün mountain, but the herders, made to carry the stones about 10 km, complained to the governor at Kalgan and won: their own sacred mountain, Dari Ovoo, became the main one (Charleux). Altan Ovoo in Dariganga is still one of the most venerated mountains in Mongolia (Charleux).

## Joining Mongolia, 1912

After Outer Mongolia declared independence in December 1911, the officials of the Dariganga herds petitioned to join the new state on 3 March 1912, and their request was confirmed in early July (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, “1911 Restoration,” p. 471). Chinese Wikipedia frames the same months differently, saying the pasture was taken over early in 1912 by the forces of the Jebtsundamba government and merged into Setsen Khan aimag. A Mongolian encyclopedia has the district’s head submitting already in 1911 (Mongoltoli). The sources differ on whether this was a petition or a takeover, and on the year.

The Republic of China sent Jodbajab to take Dariganga back. He was a Chahar official (1876–1947) and a firm Qing loyalist who had been amban of the Dariganga herds in 1908 (Atwood, *Young Mongols and Vigilantes*, pp. 593–594). By the 1920s he had joined the Inner Mongolian People’s Revolutionary Party, and he is said to have attended its First Congress in 1925 (pp. 510, 593). His troops were dispersed, and he was arrested on 28 August 1912 and held until the amnesty that followed the Kyakhta treaty of 1915 (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 132; *Young Mongols and Vigilantes*, p. 594).

The Bogd Khan’s government then took over the dues that had gone to the Qing emperor, though many Dariganga herders escaped them by enrolling in the Great Shabi, the Jebtsundamba Khutugtu’s personal estate (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 132). The theocratic government also gave Dariganga a hereditary nobility it had never had, turning appointive offices there into hereditary fiefs (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, “Jibzundamba Khutugtu, Eighth,” p. 270, and “theocratic period,” p. 533).

## Claimed for Chahar, 1913–1924

From here the sources describe two different Dariganga.

**On the ground**, Dariganga stayed under Outer Mongolian administration. Atwood writes that the Kyakhta treaty of 1915 left autonomous Outer Mongolia with Dariganga while taking away Inner Mongolia and Hulunbuir (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, “Kyakhta Trilateral Treaty,” p. 324).

**On paper**, China treated it as Chinese land outside the autonomy:

- Chinese Wikipedia reads the Sino-Russian declaration of 1913 and its annex as defining autonomous Outer Mongolia by the old jurisdictions of the Qing officials at Urga, Uliastai and Khovd. Dariganga fell outside those jurisdictions, so in that reading it was to be governed directly by China.
- In 1914 the Beiyang government assigned Dariganga to the Chahar Special Region. In practice Outer Mongolia kept it throughout, and the Chahar military governor never exercised his authority there (Chinese Wikipedia).
- The US State Department’s International Boundary Study No. 173 counts it as part of Chinese Inner Mongolia through 1924.
- Atwood’s later work shows the office surviving under its old name. The Chahar post of amban of the “Three Banners and Dariganga” herds passed from Serengdongrub to Jodbajab, although Dariganga itself had been Mongolian territory since 1911 (Atwood, *Young Mongols and Vigilantes*, p. 510 and n. 16).

The Timeline Map draws Dariganga inside Mongolia for these years, hatched in China’s colour as assigned to Chahar but not administered.

## Chinese occupation and the revolution, 1919–1921

When Chinese troops ended Outer Mongolia’s autonomy in 1919, Dariganga fell to China with the rest of Outer Mongolia, and the map draws it with China for 1920. After Ungern-Sternberg’s White Russian troops took Urga in February 1921, China again sent Jodbajab to secure Dariganga. In August 1921 a task force of Soviet Kalmyk troops, helped by Mongolian partisans, drove him out once more (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 132).

## In the Mongolian People’s Republic

The revolutionary government reorganised Dariganga as a banner with an elective government. Atwood dates this to 1924, while the Mongolian encyclopedia makes it a banner of Khan Khentii Uul aimag from 1925. It was attached to the Eastern province in 1931, and moved to the new Sükhbaatar province in 1941 (Atwood) or 1942 (Mongoltoli) (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 132, and “Sükhebaatur province,” p. 523; Mongoltoli).

In the 1930s it lay on the MPR’s guarded frontier. In late 1928 the border stations from Gurwanbayan south and west through Tamsag, Yegüzer Temple (Erdenetsagaan), Dariganga and Zamyn-Üüd were taken from the internal security service and handed to regular army troops, to guard against Japanese influence from across the frontier (Atwood, *Young Mongols and Vigilantes*, p. 937). Atwood notes that in the 1930s the Dariganga were treated simply as Khalkha, and that they were counted as a separate subethnic group only from the 1956 census (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 132).

## Claimed by Mengjiang, 1936–1945

Japanese maps of the Mengjiang period drew Dariganga inside Prince De’s territory. The Nakamura map of 1941 loops the Mengjiang border round it, inside the Outer Mongolian line, and a modern map of the border’s history draws the Mongolian Military Government’s claim of May 1936 the same way (see the Timeline Map’s notes). The MPR held it throughout, and the map draws it as disputed for 1936–1945.

## Settled

Chinese Wikipedia records that Dariganga took part in the 1945 referendum on Outer Mongolian independence, which the Nationalist government recognised. Under the 1962 boundary treaty between the People’s Republic of China and the Mongolian People’s Republic, which fixed the border along the line each side actually held, Dariganga formally became part of Mongolia (Chinese Wikipedia). Those two steps closed the dispute that began with the petition of 1912.
