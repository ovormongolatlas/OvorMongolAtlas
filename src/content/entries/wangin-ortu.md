Wangin Ortu, “the King’s Camp,” was the seat of the princes of the Sünid Right Banner (West Sünid) and the home of [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub), Prince De. Chinese sources call it 德王府, “Prince De’s mansion.” See the [ruling house of West Sünid](/entry/west-sunid-ruling-house) for the family that built and held it.

## Building

Jagchid credits the palace to Demchugdongrub’s father, Namjilwangchug, who inherited the banner in 1863. Preoccupied with fengshui, he spent much of the banner’s wealth and labour on a geomantically sited palace as a symbol of his standing (Jagchid). A 2017 China News Service report says instead that De Wang’s grandfather built it for De Wang’s father, modelled on the Beijing palace, and a tourist guide text gives 1868 as the year building began (China News Service; kechaowang.com). The 1868 date is the source of this entry’s start year and comes from the weakest source cited here; Jagchid gives no year.

## The palace in the 1920s and 1930s

The palace stood on the Kalgan–Urga road. Jagchid writes that after the Kyakhta settlement of 1915 trade between Urga and Beijing by way of Kalgan grew quickly and all of it passed close to the princely residence, with motor traffic stopping overnight at Pangjiang (Jagchid, p. 16).

Sven Hedin, who reached it in November 1929, describes a Chinese-style residence with a private temple. He records that the Panchen Lama had stayed there for about a month that summer with roughly 10,000 pilgrims camped around the palace, and that the prince was away when his own party arrived (Hedin). The Welsh journalist Gareth Jones visited Demchugdongrub at the palace in July 1935 and attended a feast there honouring the mountain spirit (Jones).

Jagchid calls it a symbolic power centre of the Inner Mongolian autonomy movement in the 1930s (Jagchid). D. Zorigt dates the inauguration of the [Mongolian Army General Headquarters](/entry/inner-mongolian-army) at the palace to 12 February 1936, with the Chinggis Khan calendar and a new blue banner in place of Republic of China symbols (Zorigt, “Self-Rule Movement”). In 1936 the army’s headquarters moved from Sünid to [Jabsar](/entry/jabsar), where the [Mongolian Military Government](/entry/mongolian-military-government) was founded that May (Jagchid).

## In 1945

When Mengjiang collapsed in August 1945, former officials and young Mongol nationalists gathered in southern Sünid Right Banner. On 16 August, at the request of the commanders of the Soviet–Mongolian troops there, they moved into the palace, where they formed an Inner Mongolian People’s Committee. The congress that proclaimed the provisional government of an Inner Mongolian People’s Republic met from 9 September at the Öndör (温都尔庙) monastery, probably the one whose ruins lie 1 km west of the palace (official gazetteer of Inner Mongolia; see the [Inner Mongolian Republican Provisional Government](/entry/inner-mongolian-republican-provisional-government) entry). Khökhbaatar, drawing on the memoir of [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid), who was there, writes that the Soviet–Mongolian command for western Inner Mongolia was quartered at the palace, and that Choibalsan himself came to Sünid Right Banner that September to visit his troops (Khökhbaatar).

A CIA field report of May 1947 calls Sünid Right Banner “Prince TE’s former residence” and gives its position as 42°40′ N, 112°53′ E, about 25 km north of the building now standing (CIA-RDP82-00457R000500670006-1). The report gives coordinates in whole minutes for the banner as a whole, so it may point to the banner’s seat rather than the palace itself.

## The site today

The palace survives as the Sünid palace museum (苏尼特王府博物馆), a state museum set up on 8 October 2011, at Usutu Obo in Zhurihe town, Sünid Right Banner (iwenbo.fun). The 2017 report places it 5 km east of Zhurihe town at the foot of Usutu Obo, with the ruins of the Öndör (Wenduer) monastery 1 km to its west (China News Service). Neither gives coordinates. The map marker is at the building itself, listed on Google Maps and Google Earth as “Sonid Youqi Mansion” (plus code CWXG+7FP), which the atlas maintainer identified in October 2026; it decodes to 42.4482° N, 112.9262° E. An earlier marker, worked out from the report’s 5 km and GeoNames’ point for Zhurihe, lay about 6 km to the south-south-east and has been replaced.

On the Sino-Swedish Expedition’s sketch map of 1945, which marks a “Residence of Barun Sunit Wang” on the expedition’s route, the residence falls about 17 km north-north-east of the building when the sheet is fitted to ten towns. The fit is least reliable in this part of the sheet, which lies north of nearly all the towns used, so the map agrees with the site within its error but does not confirm it. Both period sources that give a position, this map and the CIA report of 1947, put the residence north of the present building, by about 17 and 25 km. Whether that reflects their imprecision or a residence somewhere other than the surviving palace is not settled here.
