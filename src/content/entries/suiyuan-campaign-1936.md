The Suiyuan campaign was the attempt in autumn 1936 by the Inner Mongolian forces under [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub), with Kwantung Army sponsorship and Chinese irregulars under Wang Ying, to take eastern Suiyuan from the provincial government of Fu Zuoyi. It failed. Boyd calls it the “Suiyuan Incident”; this atlas uses “Suiyuan campaign” for the whole of the fighting from August to December 1936 and treats the November battle at [Bailingmiao](/entry/bailingmiao) as its centre. The sources disagree on several points, and those are set side by side below. Most of the detail comes from one participant-historian, Sechin Jagchid, whose account is partisan and is not independently confirmed on every point.

## Background

In December 1935 the East Chahar garrison under [Li Shouxin](/entry/li-shouxin), with Yin Baoshan and Liu Jiguang, occupied six counties of northern Chahar (Baochang, Guyuan, Zhangbei, Shangyi, Shangdu and Kangbao) with little bloodshed, and a Chahar League office was set up at Zhangbei (Jagchid, p. 132). Narangerel dates the occupation of Zhangbei to 31 December 1935 and the League office to 22 January 1936; Jagchid is less exact. The Mongol Army headquarters followed on 10 February 1936 and the [Mongolian Military Government](/entry/mongolian-military-government) on 12 May (Narangerel). Atwood dates the military government to February 1936 (“Demchungdongrub, Prince”), a conflict recorded here and not resolved.

The winter of 1935 was severe: Jagchid says about 80 percent of the animals of Shilingol died, and Suiyuan imposed a grain boycott on the Mongols (p. 154). In summer 1936 the Kwantung Army set up a Special Service office at Beile-yin sume under Morishima Kadofisa, with Nakajima Manzo as assistant; two Japanese agents posed as students at the Mergen-juu temple, and Wang Jingguo's troops, disguised as Urad Front Banner peace preservation troops, attacked the temple and killed the families of Shirabdorji's opponents. The agents were released at Baotou (pp. 154 to 155). In June Demchugdongrub visited Xinjing, met Itagaki, and received from Puyi the title “Wude Qinwang” (pp. 155 to 156). The Nanjing government meanwhile named him chairman of a Chahar Mongolian Political Council on 17 July 1936, a body that was never established (pp. 157 to 158). Itagaki's review of the Mongol army and the opening of Special Service offices at Dingyuanying and Ejine followed (pp. 158 to 159).

## The pressure to act

Tanaka Ryukichi, head of the Dehua Special Service Office, pushed for action; Demchugdongrub and [Wu Heling](/entry/wu-heling) resisted, and Jagchid says Wu had Chiang Kai-shek's secret instruction to delay the Japanese (p. 159). In August Wang Daoyi's 3,000 to 4,000 bandits and irregulars from Hebei and Chahar were rushed from Shangdu against Khonggortu (Honggeertu) and were defeated; Wang was killed by his own troops. Fu Zuoyi's propaganda spoke of “twenty thousand bogus autonomous army troops” invading Taolin (p. 159). Wang Ying, the richest Suiyuan landlord, then in Tianjin, organized the Da Han Yijun (Great Chinese Upright Army) with Lei Zhongtian as deputy (pp. 159 to 160).

## The campaign

Li Shouxin's First Army was at Nanhaozhan and Demchugdongrub's Second Army was in western Chahar covering Dehua and [Jabsar](/entry/jabsar). On 5 November Demchugdongrub sent an ultimatum to Fu Zuoyi as chairman of the Chahar Mongolian Political Council, and Fu replied on 8 November (p. 161). Wang Ying's men and Li's troops gathered at Honggeertu and Nanhaozhan and marched on Taolin; both sides bogged down, and on 19 November Wang attacked Xinghe and was repelled (p. 161). Mugdenbuu's Seventh Division was placed at Bailingmiao with the retired Colonel Obama Ujiyoshi as adviser, and Tanaka meant to use it against Hohhot from the north (p. 161). Chiang Kai-shek telegraphed on 10 November to Zhang Qun and on 16 November ordered the occupation of Beile-yin sume, and he met Yan Xishan at Taiyuan on 17 November (pp. 161 to 162). Yan's claim of 23 November that the other side had “airplanes, tanks and poison gas” Jagchid calls “a complete fabrication” (p. 162).

On the evening of 23 November the 35th Army's brigade under Sun Lanfeng, coming from Wuchuan, and Wang Jingguo's Seventh Division attacked. Zhao Chengshuo advanced from Guyang, Mugdenbuu retreated, and by dawn Beile-yin sume was in Chinese hands. Jagchid says the Chinese press claims of a great victory were false: the temple was not burned but was wrecked and looted, about 100 Mongols were killed or wounded, and Yondonwangchug stayed 35 km away (pp. 162 to 163). The Mongol side fell back to Shira-Muren in Derben Keuked Banner, and a counterattack by Tanaka's Chinese troops under Obama failed. On the night of 9 December Wang Ying's men under Shi Yushan and Jin Xianzhang killed 29 Japanese, including Obama, and disarmed the Seventh Division (pp. 163 to 164). On 18 December An Huating defected and the Great Chinese Upright Army collapsed; Tanaka sent Wang Ying to Tianjin (p. 164).

## The ending

The Xi'an Incident of 12 December changed the politics. Demchugdongrub called a truce on 16 December and Wu Heling drafted the telegram; Song Zheyuan and Xiao Zhengying mediated (pp. 164 to 165). Mugdenbuu was stripped of command, Tanaka was recalled, and Colonel Moto Akira, then Morioka and then Kawazaki headed the Dehua Special Service Office; the army was reorganized (pp. 165 to 166). Boyd draws the same link between the Xi'an Incident and the end of the fighting.

## Where the sources disagree

- **Who held Bailingmiao, and when.** Jagchid has the Chinese in possession from 23 to 24 November 1936. Boyd cites a China Weekly Review headline of 28 November 1936, “Chinese Forces Capture Pailingmiao”, which agrees. Boyle's footnote says Manchurian troops captured Pailingmiao in March 1936 and that in November the Pailingmiao-based troops launched an invasion of eastern Suiyuan, repelled in December (p. 50, citing Gillin, *Warlord*, pp. 230 to 39). This atlas's earlier notes also had it retaken in late December; that sits badly with Jagchid and with the 28 November headline and is not adopted.
- **Dates.** Atwood gives “September to November 1936” for the Japanese-provoked battle (“Demchungdongrub, Prince”); Jagchid has an August attack at Honggeertu and the main fighting in November.
- **Forces.** Boyd gives a Mongol and allied force of about 16,000 in four units against about 45,000 under Fu Zuoyi. Fu's side spoke of 20,000 invaders. Jagchid gives no total.
- **Japanese role.** The Chinese press and Boyd treat Japanese support as real. Jagchid, who is concerned to show that Demchugdongrub resisted Tanaka, describes Japanese involvement as pressure from the Special Service office, and the 9 December killing of 29 Japanese shows Japanese officers were present.
