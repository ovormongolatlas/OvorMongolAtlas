This atlas's master chronology places Gungsangnorbu (Prince Gung, 貢桑諾爾布) at the start of several threads this atlas covers elsewhere in more built-out form: the Kharachin Right Banner succession, early-1900s Mongol modernizing education, the Beijing Mongolian-Tibetan School that trained a later generation of Mongol nationalists, and a Japanese-leaning turn around the 1911 Xinhai Revolution. But every specific date and detail available to this atlas for him currently comes only from "reference/web — verify" tier sources, encyclopedic anchors rather than independently citable scholarship. Per this atlas's sourcing rules, this entry is deliberately left as a stub rather than fleshed out with that material.

**Reference-tier dates, unsettled, not to be treated as fact:** birth given as either 1871 or 1872 (one account places the latter specifically at the Kharachin Right Banner palace); death given as either 1930 or 1931. This atlas's master chronology records this as an explicit, unresolved conflict (its Conflict D) and does not pick a side; the header above now displays both attested values for each endpoint rather than selecting one, and neither is asserted as more likely correct than the other. Atwood 2002, the first T1 scholarly source this atlas holds that states his dates at all, gives 1871 to 1931. That is a pairing neither of this atlas's two reference-tier candidate pairs produces on its own, and it falls inside the range the header displays. It is recorded here as one scholar's stated dates, not as a resolution: Atwood states them in passing without discussing the conflict, so this atlas does not treat him as having adjudicated it.

## The Chongzheng Academy and its graduates

Christopher Atwood's archive-based study of Inner Mongolia's interregnum decades gives Gungsangnorbu, whom he renders Güngsangnorbu and abbreviates to the commonly used "Prince Güng," dates of 1871 to 1931, and treats him principally as an educator whose institutions shaped the following generation's politics (Atwood). Atwood credits him with an important role in establishing schools in his own banner, among them the Chongzheng Academy, which he describes as the alma mater of a large proportion of Inner Mongolia's literary and political leaders, and with encouraging its graduates to continue their studies outside Inner Mongolia (Atwood).

Atwood makes that educational legacy structural rather than incidental. He argues that the activists of the [Inner Mongolian People's Revolutionary Party](/entry/inner-mongolian-peoples-revolutionary-party), though drawn from across the region, clustered into cliques defined by a shared origin and attached either to an educator or to a garrison commander, and that the largest of these was the Kharachin Right Banner group, who owed their education to Gungsangnorbu's efforts. Those regional distinctions persisted, in his account, even after members had studied in Moscow or Ulaanbaatar (Atwood). On this reading Gungsangnorbu's schools are one of the formative institutions behind the 1925 party, which is the connection this atlas's master chronology had asserted at reference tier and can now attribute to scholarship.

Atwood names specific graduates who became party figures, several of whom he places in the winter 1906 cohort Gungsangnorbu sponsored at the Tokyo Military Academy: Dechin (born 1882, Chinese name Yi Deqin, of Kharachin Middle Banner), who later led the party's old Central Committee in 1927–28; Jin Yongchang (originally Nomunbilig, later Altan or Altan-Ochir, of Kharachin Right), elected to the lower house of the Chinese Parliament on the Kuomintang ticket in 1913; Alta (Chinese name Wu Zixing, died 1926); and Algimbuu (Chinese name Guo Gaoxuan). Atwood records that Gungsangnorbu sent three female students to a women's school in Japan at the same time as that military-academy cohort (Atwood). This atlas notes, without merging the accounts, that Uchida 2008 independently describes the same 1906 moment as three girls followed by five boys from Kharachin Right, the first Mongol students in Japan (Uchida).

## Later political role

Atwood also places Gungsangnorbu in national politics well after the educational reforms. In 1924, amid Chinese plans for a three-pronged invasion of Outer Mongolia, Gungsangnorbu and Prince Lhawangburügjil of Alashan Banner, whom Atwood describes as his opponent on the right, convened the banner rulers at a Mongolian Affairs Conference (Mengshi huiyi) in Beijing; the invasion plans lapsed when the Sino-Soviet recognition treaty was signed days later (Atwood). Separately, Atwood records that when Sun Yat-sen drew up a list of participants for a conference on China's future, the Mongols he chose were from the old elite, Gungsangnorbu among them, alongside the Khalkha expatriate prince Nayantu and the Janggiya Khutugtu (Atwood). Both episodes place him, by the 1920s, as an establishment figure courted by Chinese national politics rather than as a reformer working only within his banner.
