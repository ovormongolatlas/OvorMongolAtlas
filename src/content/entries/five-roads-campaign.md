> **DRAFT ENTRY.** Built from two Mongolian-language secondary sources, both read in full, with Jagchid's English-language account of how the Inner Mongolian leagues responded. Dates, commanders and outcomes differ between them and are set side by side below rather than reconciled. The archival and monograph sources they cite have not been consulted.

The Five Roads campaign (Таван замын байлдаан, “the battle of the five roads”) was the Bogd Khanate's attempt in 1913 to drive the forces of the Republic of China out of Inner Mongolia and join it to the new Mongolian state. Mongolian historiography uses that name; Inner Mongolian historiography calls it “Үхэр жилийн үймээн” (the Ox Year Disturbance), and Chinese historiography treats it as an incursion by Outer Mongolian rebels (Myagmarsambuu).

## Background

After the Bogd Khanate declared independence in December 1911, the Barga and many Inner Mongols joined it (Myagmarsambuu). In late 1912 the government sent a delegation under the foreign minister Khanddorj to St Petersburg to buy or borrow arms in quantity, meaning to issue them in Khalkha, Khovd and Hulunbuir; Russia first agreed and then refused, saying it would itself guard Outer Mongolia (Baabar, citing Tachibana). The government's appeal to the people of Inner Mongolia to join in driving out the Chinese dates from early 1913 (Baabar).

[Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid) describes how the leagues bordering Outer Mongolia answered Urga's earlier circular, issued in the Bogd Khan's name, inviting Inner Mongolian nobles, officials and commoners to join the independence struggle. Hulunbuir joined at once. The Ulanchab nobles were "comparatively pro-Ulaanbaatar" but, under pressure from Beijing, only expressed sympathy and did not declare independence. In Shilingol only the league head, Yangsang, opposed the movement; Outer Mongolian troops took him into custody at his residence in Abaga Left Banner and imprisoned him in Urga, after which the deputy head, Sodnamrabdan of Ujumuchin Right Banner, acted for the league with a "wait-and-see" policy (Jagchid, pp. 13–14). Jagchid does not date the arrest; it falls between the declaration of independence in December 1911 and the 1913 campaign. Beijing, for its part, tried to hold the Inner Mongolian princes with the Regulations for the Treatment of Mongolia (1912), which kept their titles, ranks and banner powers intact (Jagchid, p. 14).

## The five routes

Baabar names the routes as: Чуулалт хаалга (Kalgan) and the western part; Бат хаалга, in Darkhan Muumingan banner (Bailingmiao, this atlas's [Bat-khaalag](/entry/bailingmiao)); Долоннуур (Dolonnor); Хөх хот ([Hohhot](/entry/hohhot)); and Бугат (Baotou), along the Хатан гол (the Yellow River). About 10,000 men took part, in both accounts.

The two sources organise the command differently:

- **Mongoltoli (Myagmarsambuu)** describes two main thrusts. Forces under Sonomdorj and Chagdarjav advanced from the southern edge of Tüsheet Khan aimag toward Ulaanchab and Hohhot; forces under Manlai Baatar Damdinsüren and Yalguun Baatar Sumiya advanced from Dariganga and the Egüzer monastery toward Shilingol, Juu Uda, Chahar and Dolonnor.
- **Baabar** names Damdinsüren as overall commander, with gün Chimidtseren on the Egüzer monastery route, Khaisan and the deputy minister Bavuujav on the Dariganga route, Nasan-Arvijikh on the route toward the Sönöd and Dörvöd Wang banners, and Sumiya on the Yellow River route.

Jagchid names a different pair of commanders for the eastern thrust: Urga, he writes, sent Shirun *jiangjun* (general) and Namsarai *bagatur* (hero) with an army to take Doloonnor, the religious center between the eastern Shilingol and Chahar leagues, to intimidate the Inner Mongolian leagues and banners into joining the new state (Jagchid, p. 15). Neither name appears in Baabar's or Mongoltoli's account; whether they are the same men under other names is not established here.

The map draws the five routes as arrows. They show directions of advance only: neither source gives a front line or names the places held, and the starting points are approximate (see the map's campaign note).

## Course and outcome

The sources agree on the outline: the army advanced in the spring, fought for some months, and was withdrawn late in the year under Russian pressure. They differ on the details.

| Point | Baabar | Mongoltoli (Myagmarsambuu) |
|---|---|---|
| Decision and start | Government decided on 25 January 1913; decrees around 1 February; army left Khüree in February | Troops entered Inner Mongolia at the end of the “middle spring month” of 1913 (a traditional month name, so March or April) |
| Command | Da Lama Puntsagdorj and Damdinsüren named commanders; a July 1913 decree set up a special office under the Egüzer Khutagt Galsandash | Damdinsüren appointed commander of the southern forces on 1 July 1913 |
| Fighting | Mostly small engagements, mostly won by the Mongols; an attack on Hohhot on 7 July 1913 with over a thousand men was beaten, and the Mongols fell back to “Вангийн сүм” (a prince's temple, not yet identified) | By the last month of autumn 1913 the army had “liberated most of Inner Mongolia” |
| End | In December 1913 the government promised Russia to withdraw | A Bogd Khan decree halted the fighting and withdrew the troops in November 1913 |
| Chinese forces | Larger and better armed (no figure) | 30,000–40,000, as estimated by researchers |
| Judgement | The victories were real but naive: Russia let the war run only to press China before the three-way talks, then stopped it | A major war that showed Beijing Mongolia could not be taken by force and brought it to negotiate |

Jagchid's account differs on how the eastern thrust ended. In his telling the force sent against Doloonnor had to withdraw "because of heavy pressure from Chinese troops" before it could take the town, and once it had gone the Chinese occupied Doloonnor and killed the Kanjurwa Khutugtu, a lama sympathetic to uniting the two Mongolias, a killing that "created a great disturbance among the Inner Mongols" (Jagchid, p. 15). Baabar and Atwood put the end of the campaign down to Russian pressure; Jagchid, for this front, to Chinese military pressure. The two need not exclude each other, but the sources are set side by side here rather than reconciled. Atwood records the campaign's furthest point here as "the outskirts of Dolonnuur," which agrees with Jagchid that the town itself was not taken.

Baabar also relays, from the Mongolian translation of Sven Hedin's travel account, a count of about fifty clashes, all lost by the Chinese. Hedin was not a witness to the fighting he describes, and the figure is his. Baabar adds that [Frans August Larson](/entry/frans-august-larson), asked by Yuan Shikai to mediate, went to Khüree but failed to persuade the Bogd Khan to stop (citing Larson's memoir).

After the withdrawal, Baabar writes, the Mongol forces turned on each other: Khalkha troops under Magsarjav were sent against Babuujav, whom he calls a Kharchin gün. The 1915 Kyakhta agreement then made only Outer Mongolia autonomous, leaving Inner Mongolia to China.
