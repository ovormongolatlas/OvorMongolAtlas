Owen Lattimore is in this atlas as a contemporary observer and later witness, not as a source for the facts of Mengjiang-era politics. He wrote about the Inner Mongolian autonomy movement while it was happening, knew several of its figures personally, and in 1983 looked back on them in a filmed interview. His accounts are set against the Mongol-side and archival sources the atlas leans on elsewhere, and where later scholarship has corrected him, that is recorded.

## Life and career

**Background, not yet checked against a source held by this atlas.** The outline in this paragraph is general knowledge and is marked as such until it is verified. Lattimore was born in Washington, D.C., on 29 July 1900, spent much of his childhood in China, and was educated in England; he never attended university as a student. He worked for a British trading firm in northern China in the 1920s (confirmed in the 1983 interview below), travelled overland to Xinjiang, studied for a year at Harvard, and from about 1929 turned to Manchuria and Inner Mongolia. His books include *The Desert Road to Turkestan* (1928), *High Tartary* (1930), *Manchuria: Cradle of Conflict* (1932), *The Mongols of Manchuria* (1934), *Inner Asian Frontiers of China* (1940) and *Mongol Journeys* (1941). He edited *Pacific Affairs* from 1934 to 1941, was Chiang Kai-shek's American political adviser at Chongqing from 1941, taught at Johns Hopkins University until 1963, and then held the chair of Chinese studies at the University of Leeds. He died on 31 May 1989. In 1950 Senator Joseph McCarthy accused him of being a Soviet agent, which Lattimore denied; he was later indicted for perjury over his testimony, and the charges were dropped in 1955. That episode is background to how his work was received in the United States and is not otherwise used by this atlas.

## What he says in the 1983 interview

Caroline Humphrey interviewed Lattimore on camera on 21 May 1983. The atlas holds two timestamped summaries of the recording (Parts 1 and 2) and a machine-generated caption transcript of the later part of the tape, which covers roughly 1:24 to 2:00 of the video. The caption is verbatim but rough: it garbles names, and it reads some sentences wrongly. Where it and the written summary differ, the summary is used for names and the caption for wording, and the difference is noted. Lattimore was speaking from memory some fifty years after the events, so the interview is treated as his recollection and not as a record.

On his own career he says he is hard to label as historian, anthropologist or geographer, never went to university, and came to Mongolia by accident: he left a British firm in China to travel on his own, spent a winter in Peking learning Mongolian, and then travelled for years in Inner Mongolia with a companion from the far west of Outer Mongolia whom he calls Arash. Arash had worked for the Sven Hedin expedition and, with his brother, a lama shaman, had fled to Inner Mongolia after the brother's patron, a counter-revolutionary, was killed. Lattimore says this friendship let him see Mongolian life from the level of a working caravan hand, the one culture he entered below his usual upper-middle-class position (Part 2, 0:08).

On Inner Mongolia in the early 1930s he describes Chinese railways reaching the edge of the steppe and Chinese settlers pressing in, with princes bribed into selling banner land that traditionally belonged to the commoners collectively, and with Mongols who resisted being labelled bandits. He also describes thousands of refugees from the Mongolian People's Republic in Inner Mongolia in this period, who went back as the republic began to prosper (Part 1, 0:22 and 0:33).

He recalls three Mongols at length:

- **Demchugdongrub (“De Wang”).** Lattimore calls him the leader of the non-Marxist, non-communist Inner Mongolian nationalist movement. During the Japanese occupation, he says, De Wang knowingly kept young concealed communists on his staff and did not hand them over, on the principle that he did not care about the political affiliations of anyone who would work for Mongolian independence. His aim was not to detach Inner Mongolia from China or to let it fall to the Japanese, but to win autonomy, including control of taxation and education. Lattimore adds that the Chinese Nationalists would not back him, the communists did not back him either, and the more conservative princes, who disliked his modernizing, were more likely to make deals with Chinese provincial governors or the Japanese. He credits him with schools that made more young Inner Mongols literate than at any earlier time. On religion, he was Buddhist the way an upper-class Englishman is nominally Church of England: he attended occasionally and did not lean on it heavily. (The caption reads as though Lattimore called him an Anglican; the written summary says “nominally a Buddhist,” and the sentence in context is an analogy.) After the Japanese surrender, Lattimore says, De Wang tried to escape into independent Mongolia, was returned to the Chinese communists, tried as a traitor, sentenced to death, and given a life sentence, and was later released and worked as librarian of a Mongol library he had himself created. Lattimore calls him “a very fine man” and says he was honoured to be considered a friend rather than a foreign expert (Part 2, 0:14; caption 1:24 to 1:30). See the [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub) entry for the other sources on the same events; the claim that he knowingly protected concealed communists is not found in the other sources used here.
- **[Ulanhu](/entry/ulanhu).** Lattimore says the name means “red child”, a term of endearment for a newborn in both Mongolian and Chinese, and that its first element echoes Ulyanov, Lenin's family name, which a young man in Peking could get away with because the Chinese did not catch it. He comes from the Tümed of Hohhot, settled Mongols, and as a student and early activist did not know Mongolian, and learned it later without becoming fluent. Lattimore's explanation for his political success is that he was a desirable acquisition for the Chinese Communist Party as a minority member who was an enthusiastic party man; he was eclipsed in the 1960s like many senior communists and has since been rehabilitated (Part 2, 0:21). The etymology is Lattimore's own and is not confirmed by any source in this atlas.
- **The [Dilowa Khutugtu](/entry/dilowa-khutugtu) (“Diluv Khutagt”, in the summary).** A reincarnate lama from western Mongolia, better educated in Tibetan than in Mongolian, whom Lattimore met in Peking. In the transcript the lama is not named; the summary supplies the name. He was governor-general of the Uliastai region in the Altai, was arrested in the 1930s purges and accused of spying for the Japanese, was given a suspended sentence and fled across the border to Inner Mongolia, where he became a close friend of De Wang. The Japanese took him to Japan after they seized Peking and tried to make him a puppet. He got back through Shanghai and Hong Kong to Chongqing, where Lattimore, then adviser to Chiang Kai-shek, found him in 1941 and had him live in his quarters. After 1945 he went by sea to India and then to Tibet, where he became part of the entourage of the infant Dalai Lama, and from Beijing he was sent by Tibetans to report on the civil war. Lattimore arranged for him to come to the United States, where he joined the Mongolian seminar at Johns Hopkins, and he died of cancer in New York. Lattimore and Fujiko Isono published his memoirs and autobiography, recorded with Lattimore's son (Part 2, 0:24 to 0:35; caption 1:34 to 1:48).

Two shorter points come up in the same recording. Lattimore describes a Mongolian historian he calls Dalai, a colleague of long standing, whose father kept a dead horse's skull on a mountain ledge as a mark of respect; the transcript and summary spell the names inconsistently, so the identification with a particular historian is left open here. And he says he would like to see the place of the Mongol conquests in world history treated beyond the Soviet view of Chinggis Khan as simply destructive and the Chinese view of him as a guarantor of trade routes (Part 2, 0:39 and 0:47).

## Lattimore in this atlas's other entries

- **1935, “On the Wickedness of Being Nomads.”** He names Demchugdongrub as the movement's best-known leader, compares him to “an Inner Mongolian Roosevelt,” and ties the movement's timing to the founding of Manchukuo in 1932 (see [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub) and [Mongolian Political Affairs Council](/entry/mongolian-political-affairs-council)).
- **1935 interviews with Mongols in Japanese service.** As reported by Nakami, he first got standard pro-Japanese answers and then something different; the atlas has not read Lattimore's own text for this (see [Japanese pan-Mongol strategy](/entry/japanese-pan-mongol-strategy)).
- **Yan Xishan's councils.** Boyle records Lattimore's view that the pressure on Chahar and the rival Mongol councils came from the “Shansi-Suiyuan interests” of Yan Xishan (see [Jabsar](/entry/jabsar)).
- **Merse.** Lattimore's account of Merse's death as an assassination is disputed, and Nakami corrects it (see [Merse](/entry/merse)).
- **1938, “The Geographical Factor in Mongol History.”** A regional view of Chahar and the Ulanchab League (see [Kalgan](/entry/kalgan)).
