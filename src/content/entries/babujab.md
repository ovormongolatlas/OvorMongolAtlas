Babujab (1870–1916) was a Mongol from Monggoljin, the Tümed Left Banner of Josotu League (today’s Fuxin), who lived as a bandit leader in eastern Inner Mongolia from the time of the Russo-Japanese War [1, 2]. He fought successively for Japan, for independent Mongolia and for the Chinese monarchists who wanted the Qing back [1]. He was killed in 1916, but his band went on fighting for another year [1, 2, 3].

*A note on sources.* The entry draws on Christopher Atwood’s encyclopedia [1] and *Young Mongols and Vigilantes* [2], [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid)’s memoir [3], Tachibana Makoto’s archive-based study [4] and Baabar’s essay [5]. The one full study, Nakami Tatsuo’s, has not been consulted (see the open questions).

## In the Five Roads campaign (1913)

In January 1913 the Mongolian war ministry assigned Babujab, with Khaisan, to the Dariganga route of the army it was sending into Inner Mongolia [4]. Atwood lists him first among the Inner Mongolian commanders of the [Five Roads campaign](/entry/five-roads-campaign) [1]. Baabar calls him Bavuujav, a deputy minister and a Kharchin *gün* (duke) [5]; Atwood and Jagchid call him a bandit from Monggoljin [1, 3].

## Against the settlement (1913–1916)

When Russian pressure ended the campaign, Babujab refused to accept the outcome, and his band disturbed the Sino-Mongolian frontier until 1917 [1]. Baabar writes that Khalkha troops under Magsarjav were sent against him [5]. After the Qing abdication, Qing loyalists in the northeast hired him to restore the dynasty [2]. Jagchid places him among the conservative eastern Mongols who, under Japanese protection, kept up contact with the Manchu Prince Su (Shanqi) and his loyalist party, the Zongshe dang, at Dairen. Babujab, he writes, had three aims: to attack the Chinese-farmed districts on Mongol land and so win the herdsmen, to restore the Manchu throne and so win the Mongol nobility, and to work with the Japanese army to strengthen his own forces. His operations covered most of eastern Inner Mongolia and the districts bordering it [3].

He was killed in 1916 by a stray bullet during a major offensive against Linxi, a strategically placed Chinese town in Juu Uda League [3]. Jagchid writes that his followers then disbanded, and that during the rising [Üjümüchin](/entry/ujumuchin) and the other eastern Shilingol banners were in turmoil [3]. Atwood’s later account has his second-in-command, Sebjingge, carrying on north and occupying Hailar in 1917 [2].

## Afterwards

His former chief of staff, Duke Fushengge of Baarin, joined the White Russian ataman Grigorii Semenov with about 800 men and in 1919 helped bring the pan-Mongol movement at Dauriia Station into being [1]. The Daur nationalist [Merse](/entry/merse) later cited Babujab’s Japanese dealings as evidence that Japanese “pan-Asianism” hid designs on Mongolia; the [Japanese pan-Mongol strategy](/entry/japanese-pan-mongol-strategy) entry has the context.

## Open questions

- **His rank and banner.** A Monggoljin bandit (Atwood, Jagchid [1, 2, 3]) or a Kharchin duke and deputy minister (Baabar [5]).
- **What became of his band.** Disbanded after his death (Jagchid [3]) or carried on under Sebjingge to Hailar in 1917 (Atwood [2]); the two may describe different parts of it.
- **The full study.** Nakami Tatsuo, “Babujab and His Uprising: Re-examining the Inner Mongol Struggle for Independence,” *Memoirs of the Research Department of the Toyo Bunko* 57 (1999), is the next source to get.
