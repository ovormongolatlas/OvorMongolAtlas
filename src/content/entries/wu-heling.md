Wu Heling (Mongol name Ünenbayan, born 1896) was a Mongol politician from the Kharachin Right Banner of the Josotu League [3, 4]. In the late 1920s he spoke for the Mongol Delegation in Beiping, and through the 1930s he worked between the Mongol leagues and the Chinese government in Nanjing, often against [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub). He then held senior office in almost every [Mengjiang](/entry/mengjiang) government, and in 1949 he was speaker of the Mongolian Congress of Demchugdongrub’s last government at Alashan, where he talked its leaders out of going into exile. The years above run from his birth to the last of his activity in the sources used here; his death is not established.

*A note on sources.* Most of what is known here comes from [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid), who was close to Wu in two ways. Wu was the husband of Jagchid’s father’s niece (Jagchid’s elder sister, in a biography of Jagchid published by National Chengchi University [5]), and Jagchid later served as his secretary and then became his factional opponent. Jagchid’s portrait carries both the intimacy and the friction of that relationship [1]. Christopher Atwood’s archive-based history of the 1920s [3] gives an outside view of his early career, and the list of his Mengjiang offices comes from a modern academic summary [2].

## Early life

He was the eldest son of Lamajab, who had organized the Mongol volunteers that fought the [Jindandao](/entry/jindandao-incident) (“Golden Elixir Sect”) rebels and protected the western half of Kharachin. Wu studied at the Beijing College of Law and Politics and graduated from the Chinese literature department of Peking University [1]. He then worked in Beijing as a teacher at the Mongolian and Tibetan Academy and a clerk in the government’s Mongolian and Tibetan Department, the bureaucracy that ran Mongol affairs under the warlord governments [3]. He was five or six years older than Demchugdongrub; Jagchid describes both as conservatives set against the leftist [Inner Mongolian People’s Revolutionary Party](/entry/inner-mongolian-peoples-revolutionary-party) who, being strong-willed, could never work together easily [1].

## The Mongol Delegation (1928)

In the late 1920s, during the Kuomintang’s Northern Expedition, a Mongol Delegation formed in Beiping, and Wu Heling was the Josotu League’s delegate to it. The Beiping Mongol elders, among them Jagchid’s father Lubsangchoijur and Ayulugei, agreed to entrust “the trustworthy youngster Wu Heling” with organizing it, and Prince Gungsangnorbu endorsed the plan. The delegation chose Wu as its spokesman after Prince Sodnamrabdan of the Shilingol League backed him [1]. Jagchid, then a junior high school student whose father was active in the same circles, saw this at first hand.

Atwood describes the same episode from the side of the party’s revolutionaries. When the Nationalists took Beiping in 1928, the party men Mandaltu and Buyantai, from Wu’s own district of Kharachin Right, arrived to take over the Mongolian and Tibetan Department. Wu called on them twice and was twice refused; his card came back with a note calling him one of the “running dogs of the dukes and princes.” They set the police on the delegation, but Wu’s connections in the city were better, and the police chief promised to protect it [3]. Wu chose delegates from the banner ministries and former Beijing officials, none of them reigning nobles or old party men, and positioned himself as a centrist between reactionaries and radicals. The delegation reached Nanjing on 1 December 1928, where it was welcomed with a large ceremony [3].

As spokesman, Wu put the delegation’s main demands to the new Nationalist government: abolish the newly created special-region provinces of Rehe, Chahar and Suiyuan, and set up a Mongolian Regional Political Committee instead. The government refused, and in 1928 it made those regions provinces over Mongol objections [1]. In Nanjing the delegation and the Nationalist Party Mongols of Bai Yunti blamed each other, the one calling the other “feudal remnants” and the other calling its rivals spineless; Atwood traces the split between the Bai Yunti and Wu Heling factions to the end of Nationalist rule in Inner Mongolia [3].

## Mediator in Nanjing (1930s)

In April 1932 he was one of the government’s Mongol delegates to the Conference on National Calamities at Luoyang [3]. Through the 1930s Wu headed the Nanjing Office of the Mongolian League and Banner Delegates and sat on the Mongolian-Tibetan Affairs Commission, a habitual go-between for Mongol leaders and the central government [1]. The role set him repeatedly against Demchugdongrub. According to Jagchid, the two clashed again and again, although circumstances sometimes forced them to work together, and Demchugdongrub once argued directly against what Jagchid calls “Wu Heling’s great accomplishment,” the Organizational Law of Mongolian Leagues, Tribes, and Banners [1]. Some of Demchugdongrub’s circle suspected Wu of being too close to the Chinese government to be trusted. Jagchid neither confirms nor dismisses the suspicion.

## Office under Mengjiang (1936–1944)

Wu held a long series of senior posts in Mengjiang’s governments. They included deputy general secretary of the [Mongolian Military Government](/entry/mongolian-military-government) (1936–1937), chairman of the senate (1937–1939 and 1940–1941), and chairman of the State Administrative Council (1941–1944), among others [2].

From 1938 he spent a period in Japan, which Jagchid describes as an exile arranged by the Japanese advisor [Kanai Shoji](/entry/kanai-shoji) to remove him from Mengjiang politics, under the pretext of deepening his knowledge of Japan. Jagchid went with him as his personal secretary, and the two studied together at Waseda University. Jagchid found the working relationship difficult and resigned after about a year and a half [1].

## The pragmatist (1948–1949)

After Mengjiang collapsed, Wu stayed in Mongol politics, and twice, a year apart, he worked against the younger radicals of Jagchid’s circle. At the National Assembly in Nanjing in March 1948 he was drafted into the caucus that polished the Mongol delegates’ joint constitutional proposal. According to Jagchid, he used the occasion to dismiss the demands of the [Mongolian Youth Alliance](/entry/mongolian-youth-alliance) as “unrealistic” and branded Jagchid and his group a “lunatic fringe” [1].

In 1949 he joined Demchugdongrub’s last attempt at self-rule, the [Mongolian Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-autonomous-government-alashan) based at Dingyuanying in Alashan, and at the delegate conference of August 1949 he was elected speaker of its 31-member Mongolian Congress [1, 2]. When the question arose of retreating through Tibet to India to form a government in exile, Wu argued that the plan was unrealistic and urged Prince Damzana to think it over practically. That turned Damzana, He Zhaolin and in the end [Bai Haifeng](/entry/bai-haifeng) against the exile plan, which Demchugdongrub and Jagchid had favored [1]. The Alashan government’s entry gives the debate in full.

Jagchid casts Wu in both episodes as the pragmatic broker working against the young radicals. That judgment comes from a man who was both his kinsman and his opponent.

## An unverified allegation

Shortly after the Alashan debate, Bayankhan told Jagchid privately that Wu “certainly [had] been working for Yan Xishan ever since the days of the Batukhaalag autonomy movement,” that he had placed himself between the central government and the Mongol leagues and banners, that during the Mengjiang period he had been doing Chiang Kai-shek’s bidding, and that he surely kept direct ties to the central government. Jagchid adds his own qualifier: the claim, “though very plausible, will perhaps always remain unverified” [1]. The atlas records it as an allegation that Bayankhan made and Jagchid relayed.

## Open questions

- **His death.** Not established by the sources used here.
- **His tie to Jagchid.** Married to Jagchid’s father’s niece [1] or to Jagchid’s elder sister [5].
- **Bayankhan’s allegation.** Whether Wu worked for Yan Xishan and Chiang Kai-shek throughout, as Bayankhan claimed. Jagchid calls it plausible but unverified.
