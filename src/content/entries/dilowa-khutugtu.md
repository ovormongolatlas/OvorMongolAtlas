The Dilowa Khutugtu (also written Diluwa, Diluv Khutagt, Dilowa Gegeen) was a high-ranking “living Buddha” of western Outer Mongolia who left the Mongolian People's Republic for Inner Mongolia and became [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub)'s closest friend among the Outer Mongolian refugees. The atlas has two friendly witnesses: [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid), who knew him through Demchugdongrub's circle, and [Owen Lattimore](/entry/owen-lattimore), who knew him in Peking, Chongqing and the United States. No source in hand gives his personal name or his dates of birth and death, so the years above are the span in which the atlas's sources document his activity, not his life. Both sources say he died in the United States; Lattimore places it after 1963.

## Who he was

Jagchid's note describes him as a high-ranking “living Buddha” under the Jalkhanza Khutugtu, the most important religious leader of the two western leagues of Outer Mongolia under the Jebtsundamba, and says he was reported to have been in Jalkhanza's entourage during a visit to Beijing. About a decade later, Jagchid writes, he fled to Inner Mongolia with about a hundred families of his disciples and settled within the territory of Demchugdongrub's banner, where he became one of the prince's closest associates (Jagchid, pp. 19 to 20).

Lattimore, in a 1983 filmed interview, gives a fuller portrait. The lama was from western Mongolia, better educated in Tibetan than Mongolian, and genuinely pious: unlike many high lamas he did not smoke, drink or keep women. In pre-revolutionary Mongolia he was made governor-general of the Uliastai region in the Altai, which brought him into contact with most of the leading figures of the 1920s and 1930s. When the purges began he was arrested and accused of spying for the Japanese, was given a suspended sentence and told to go home, and on the way slipped with a disciple to the Inner Mongolian border and crossed it as a refugee. Lattimore calls him a father figure of the refugees there, and says he led an active secular life alongside his religious observance (Lattimore, 1983 interview, Part 2, 0:24 to 0:35, and caption 1:34 to 1:48). Lattimore also says the lama told him more than once that there would be no reincarnation of himself.

## With Demchugdongrub, 1935 to 1936

In June 1935 the Kwantung Army placed a Special Service Office at Sünid Right Banner; one of its members, Yamamoto Nobunaga, was assigned to work among the Dilowa Khutugtu's circle and the settlers from Outer Mongolia and to gather intelligence on Outer Mongolia (Jagchid, about pp. 129 to 130). In mid-November 1935, after the Kwantung Army sent a formal invitation, Demchugdongrub discussed it with “his best friend” the Dilowa Khutugtu, and the two agreed to accept the risks. They flew to Hsinking (Changchun) together, with Major Shishiura, Nakajima Manzo and the interpreter Altanwachir, in the airplane the Kwantung Army had given Demchugdongrub (Jagchid, pp. 130 to 132). The Dilowa Khutugtu was also with Demchugdongrub and Damrinsurung at the opening of the Chahar League office at Zhangbei, and afterward, when Demchugdongrub had snubbed [Wu Heling](/entry/wu-heling), the lama came to visit Wu and spoke to him about the prince's cold manner (Jagchid, pp. 132 to 134). In a discussion of possible political lines that Jagchid reports from Liu Jianhua, Demchugdongrub rejected the Outer Mongolian route, saying that reports from the Dilowa Gegeen and other Outer Mongolian refugees showed that the communized state persecuted lay nobles and lamas (Jagchid, p. 108).

## The Ninth Jebtsundamba mission and the road to Chongqing

Jagchid's account, in the chapter on Japanese attempts to use Mongol religion, is that the Kwantung Army and the Japanese army in Mongolia wanted to find the Ninth Jebtsundamba Khutugtu, because that would give them great political influence over the Mongols north of the Gobi. Just before the Pacific War, after hearing that the ninth incarnation had probably been found in Tibet, the Japanese sent the Dilowa Khutugtu to bring him to Inner Mongolia. He accepted because of his beliefs and his hatred of the communist regime in Outer Mongolia, and he told Demchugdongrub, who argued against it: the Jebtsundamba line, he thought, had ended with the Eighth, and the search would revive a Manchu-era policy of religious restraint on the Mongols. The lama went anyway, by Japanese arrangement from Kalgan to Beiping, Tianjin and Shanghai and by sea to Hong Kong, where he bought a ticket to Calcutta. Jagchid says British intelligence found him there, and the British and Chinese services, taking advantage of his lack of English, Chinese or Cantonese, put him on a plane to Chongqing without his knowing the destination (Jagchid, pp. 271 to 272). In a note Jagchid adds that the lama pretended to be overjoyed at arriving, met his old friend Lattimore, then Chiang Kai-shek's adviser, and was made a member of the Legislative Yuan and of the National Political Council; after the war he resigned and asked to go to Tibet to see the Ninth Jebtsundamba, a request the Chinese government approved. Jagchid says he reached Tibet and met his religious goals but the political ones proved impossible (the note is cut off at that point in the text consulted).

Lattimore's account of the same journey differs. In his telling, after the Japanese took Peking they got hold of the lama and took him to Japan, honored him at Buddhist monasteries and tried to make him a puppet; he evaded them, reached Shanghai and then Hong Kong with the help of a Chinese Buddhist, and got to Chongqing, where Lattimore found him in 1941 when he went to work as Chiang's adviser and had him live in his quarters, where the two could talk in Mongolian without anyone understanding (Lattimore, 1983 interview, Part 2, 0:24, and caption 1:37 to 1:39). The two accounts agree on the route through Shanghai and Hong Kong and on the Chongqing arrival in about 1941. They differ on the purpose, on Japan, and on whether he arrived by evasion or by being put on a plane. Jagchid's version has the lama ignorant of his destination; Lattimore's has him escaping. This atlas records both and does not choose.

## After 1945

Lattimore says that after the Japanese surrender the lama went to Hong Kong and by sea to Calcutta and Tibet, where he became part of the entourage of the infant Dalai Lama; the Tibetans sent him back to China to report on the civil war, and he was in Beijing when Chiang's government collapsed. Lattimore then arranged for him to fly to the United States, where he joined the Mongolian seminar at Johns Hopkins University. Jagchid has the same end from the other side: before the situation in Beiping became extremely tense, the lama, who had gone to Nanjing intending to reach Tibet through India, accepted Lattimore's invitation to join the Johns Hopkins program, and Demchugdongrub, seeing an opportunity, had Giljintei, a Shilingol legislator, write a document making the lama his personal representative with the right to carry on the movement for Mongolian independence once in the United States (Jagchid, pp. 390 to 394). Lattimore does not mention that document. The lama's work in the United States on that representation is not documented in any source consulted here. He died of cancer in New York, with Lattimore flying from England to see him shortly before (Lattimore, 1983 interview).

## The memoirs

Lattimore and Fujiko Isono published the lama's memoirs and autobiography as *The Diluv Khutagt: Memoirs and Autobiography of a Mongol Buddhist Reincarnation in Religion and Revolution* (Wiesbaden: Otto Harrassowitz, 1982). Lattimore says it contains his political memoirs in facsimile with translation, an autobiography recorded in sessions in which the lama spoke Mongolian and Lattimore paraphrased into English for his son, who wrote it down, and a description of his monastery and territory with a map drawn from memory. Atwood's *Encyclopedia* lists the volume as further reading under several entries, and *Young Mongols and Vigilantes* cites it for details of pilgrimage and the Panchen Lama's Beijing visit (pp. 131 and 177 of the memoir). This atlas has not read the memoirs, so everything above comes from Jagchid, Lattimore's interview and the bibliographic notes.
