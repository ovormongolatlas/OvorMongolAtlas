Gungsangnorbu (Prince Gung, 貢桑諾爾布) was the ruling prince of Kharachin Right Banner in Josotu League and the best-known Inner Mongolian modernizer of the late Qing. His schools in Kharachin trained a large share of the Mongol politicians and writers of the next generation, and from 1912 he headed the Republic’s office for Mongolian and Tibetan affairs in Beijing.

**Dates.** His birth is given as 1871 or 1872 and his death as 1930 or 1931. Atwood gives 1871–1931 without discussing the question (Atwood, p. 83). Jagchid puts his death in Beiping in “the spring of 1930” in two places and in 1931 in a third (see below). The earlier figures come from encyclopedic references the atlas has not been able to check, so the header shows both years for each end.

## Reforms in Kharachin Right Banner

Atwood ties the reforms to the banner’s money. Kharachin Right had a large income from leased land, enough political weight to keep its rights over those leases, and fairly competent accounts, so it could afford New Policies institutions: modern schools with Japanese teachers, a new police force, and modern post and telegraph offices (Atwood, p. 83). After Japan’s victory over Russia in 1905 put Josotu League in the Japanese sphere, Gungsangnorbu used his connections with Japanese officers to get Kharachin students into schools in Tokyo (Atwood, pp. 83–84).

## The Chongzheng Academy and its graduates

Christopher Atwood's archive-based study of Inner Mongolia's interregnum decades gives Gungsangnorbu, whom he renders Güngsangnorbu and abbreviates to the commonly used "Prince Güng," dates of 1871 to 1931, and treats him principally as an educator whose institutions shaped the following generation's politics (Atwood). Atwood credits him with an important role in establishing schools in his own banner, among them the Chongzheng Academy, which he describes as the alma mater of a large proportion of Inner Mongolia's literary and political leaders, and with encouraging its graduates to continue their studies outside Inner Mongolia (Atwood).

Atwood makes that educational legacy structural rather than incidental. He argues that the activists of the [Inner Mongolian People's Revolutionary Party](/entry/inner-mongolian-peoples-revolutionary-party), though drawn from across the region, clustered into cliques defined by a shared origin and attached either to an educator or to a garrison commander, and that the largest of these was the Kharachin Right Banner group, who owed their education to Gungsangnorbu's efforts. Those regional distinctions persisted, in his account, even after members had studied in Moscow or Ulaanbaatar (Atwood). On this reading Gungsangnorbu's schools are one of the formative institutions behind the 1925 party, which is the connection this atlas's master chronology had asserted at reference tier and can now attribute to scholarship.

Atwood names the five Kharachin students whom Gungsangnorbu placed in the Tokyo Military Academy in winter 1906:

- **Engkhebürin** (Wu Enhe, 1886–1954), later a parliamentarian and educator, president of the Mongolian and Tibetan Technical School in Beijing from 1924 to 1929;
- **Temgetü** (Wang Ruichang, 1887–1939), who pioneered Mongolian-language printing with movable type;
- **Nomunbilig** (Jin Yongchang, later AltanOchir), who moved to Tokyo Imperial University’s School of Agriculture and Forestry and in 1913 was elected to the Chinese Parliament on the Kuomintang ticket;
- **Dechin** (Yi Deqin, born 1882), of Kharachin Middle Banner, a graduate of the Chongzheng Academy who later led the Inner Mongolian People’s Revolutionary Party’s old Central Committee in 1927–28;
- **Yu Hengshan**.

At the same time he sent three girls to a women’s school in Japan (Atwood, p. 85 and n. 20). Uchida 2008 independently describes the same moment as three girls followed by five boys from Kharachin Right, the first Mongol students in Japan (Uchida). An earlier version of this entry also counted Alta (Wu Zixing) and Algimbuu (Guo Gaoxuan) in the Tokyo group; Atwood does not. Alta studied at the Chongzheng Academy and then at the Army Academy for Princes and Nobles in Beijing (Atwood, p. 1009).

## 1911–1912

Atwood writes that after the 1911 revolution Gungsangnorbu rejected joining the new government in Khalkha under the Jebtsundamba Khutugtu. Through 1912 he looked first to Qing loyalism and then to a joint Manchurian–Mongolian independence, and finally, like the other East Mongolian princes, concluded that the only alternative to the Khalkha theocracy was Yuan Shikai’s Republic. In September 1912 he accepted office as director general of the Republic’s Mongolian and Tibetan Affairs Bureau, renamed the Mongolian and Tibetan Affairs Department in 1914, which carried on the Qing policy of managing the Inner Asian nobles and clergy with rewards and punishments (Atwood, p. 84). Jagchid tells the same months differently: Gungsangnorbu called a conference at Ulankhada and looked for Japanese help toward Inner Mongolian independence, and gave up only when Japan came to terms with Yuan, who then won him over with the appointment (Jagchid, p. 14; see below). The two accounts agree on the outcome and on Japan’s place in his plans; they differ in what they stress.

One of his first acts in office, Atwood writes, was to merge the Qing court’s schools for Mongols and Tibetans into a free public Mongolian and Tibetan School (MengZang xuexiao), funded and accredited by the Education Ministry. Its headmaster was Dashou (born 1870), a Manchu of the Plain Red Banner; it moved in 1916 and was renamed the Mongolian and Tibetan Technical School in 1918 (Atwood, p. 84). Jagchid instead has Gungsangnorbu urging the Mongolian members of parliament in 1913 to found a Mongolian-Tibetan Academy, staffed with students he had sent to Japan (Jagchid, p. 15). Whether these describe one institution at different stages is not stated by either source.

## In Jagchid's *The Last Mongol Prince*

Sechin Jagchid's biography of [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub) touches Gungsangnorbu in its opening chapters. This is not the article on Gungsangnorbu that gates this entry (see below), and Jagchid writes as a participant: his own father, Lubsangchoijur, served Gungsangnorbu as his personal representative.

- **Boxer year, 1900.** As jasag of Kharachin Right Banner, Gungsangnorbu adopted a policy of “protection for the land and tranquility for the people” and kept the Boxers out of his territory (Jagchid, p. 9).
- **Modernization.** By 1902 he had begun “a modest program of modernization in his own banner,” following the pattern of the Meiji Restoration; sometime between 1902 and 1908 he returned from a visit to Japan, founded a boys’ school, a girls’ school and a military school, and sent young men and women to study in Japan (Jagchid, pp. 9–11). Jagchid gives no year for the visit. He adds that Gungsangnorbu supported Japan in the Russo-Japanese War (p. 12).
- **1911–1912.** At the court conference on the Qing abdication he was one of the few who opposed it, with the Manchu Prince Su (Shanqi) and the Khalkha prince Nayantu. He then called princes and delegates of Josotu, Juu Uda and part of Jirim League to a conference at Ulankhada (Chifeng), argued for seeking Japanese help toward Inner Mongolian independence, and sent Lubsangchoijur to Urga to open contact with the new regime there. In Jagchid’s account nothing came of it because Japan reached an agreement with Yuan Shikai’s government and dropped him, and Yuan won him back by appointing him head of the office for Mongolian and Tibetan affairs (Jagchid, p. 14). This atlas’s master chronology, at reference tier, gives a different reason for the failure (the size of the Chinese population and factionalism among the Mongols); the two are recorded side by side.
- **1913.** As head of the Bureau (later Ministry) of Mongolian-Tibetan Affairs he urged the Mongolian members of parliament to pass a resolution founding the Mongolian-Tibetan Academy in Beijing, and staffed it with students he had earlier sent to Japan; the academy, Jagchid writes, “gradually became the headquarters for Mongolian modernization, reform, and revolution” (Jagchid, p. 15).
- **Death.** Jagchid twice puts his death in Beiping in “the spring of 1930” (pp. 42, 44) and once writes “in 1931, when Prince Gungsangnorbu died” (p. 50), so the book adds to the date conflict above rather than settling it.

## Later political role

Atwood also places Gungsangnorbu in national politics well after the educational reforms. In 1924, amid Chinese plans for a three-pronged invasion of Outer Mongolia, Gungsangnorbu and Prince Lhawangburügjil of Alashan Banner, whom Atwood describes as his opponent on the right, convened the banner rulers at a Mongolian Affairs Conference (Mengshi huiyi) in Beijing; the invasion plans lapsed when the Sino-Soviet recognition treaty was signed days later (Atwood). Separately, Atwood records that when Sun Yat-sen drew up a list of participants for a conference on China's future, the Mongols he chose were from the old elite, Gungsangnorbu among them, alongside the Khalkha expatriate prince Nayantu and the Janggiya Khutugtu (Atwood). Both episodes place him, by the 1920s, as an establishment figure courted by Chinese national politics rather than as a reformer working only within his banner.

## Still to be read

The fullest study of his life, Sechin Jagchid’s article “Prince Gungsangnorbu: Forerunner of Inner Mongolian Nationalism,” *Zentralasiatische Studien* 12 (1981), pp. 147–158, reprinted in Jagchid’s *Essays in Mongolian Studies* (Provo, Utah, 1988), pp. 207–233, has not yet been read for this entry, nor has Nasan Bayar’s case study of him. They may settle his dates.
