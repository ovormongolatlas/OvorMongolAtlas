The Sünid (also written Sönid, Sunid or Sunit) were one of the five Mongol tribes of Shilingol League, organized under the Qing as two banners, Sünid Left and Sünid Right, on the league’s dry western side [1, 2]. Jagchid writes that Shilingol was famous for the best pastures in Inner Mongolia, and that Sünid in the west and Üjümüchin in the east were especially famous for their horses; the Sünid also had the salt lake of Ereen (Erlian) on the border with Outer Mongolia [1]. Atwood places the eastern edge of the Gobi in Sünid Left Banner [2]. The Right Banner’s prince, [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub), led the Inner Mongolian autonomy movement from 1933; the [ruling house of West Sünid](/entry/west-sunid-ruling-house) has its own entry.

*A note on sources.* The entry rests on [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid)’s history of Demchugdongrub, which opens with the Sünid [1], Christopher Atwood’s encyclopedia [2], Tachibana Makoto’s archive-based study of Shilingol in 1912–1913 [3], and Isabelle Charleux’s study of the Berlin banner maps [4]. The maps above are three of the seven Sünid maps in the Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin (see below).

## Origins and submission to the Qing (1630s–1640s)

The Sünid were placed under Torubolod, eldest son of Dayan Khan, whose descendants also ruled the Chahar, Üjümüchin and Khuuchid [1]. When Ligdan Khan allied with the Ming against the Manchus, the Sünid, Üjümüchin and Khuuchid moved north to Khalkha rather than fight their kinsman, and came back south and submitted to the Manchus after his defeat [1]. Atwood dates the revolt against Ligdan to 1627 and the submission of the Sünid and the other former Chahar princes to 1637 [2].

Jagchid gives each banner its own date. The Right Flank under Susai submitted in 1638, and in 1642 Abahai (Hong Taiji) made Susai a second-rank prince (*jasag törü-yin jünwang*) and ruler of the Right Banner. Susai’s nephew Tenggis submitted with the Left Flank in 1639, married Abahai’s daughter the next year, and received the same rank in 1641, when the two Sünid banners were formed [1]. The header shows Atwood’s 1637 beside Jagchid’s 1641.

## ‘Sünid traitors’

After Abahai’s death, Tenggis quarrelled with the regent Dorgon, and in 1646 he took his Manchu wife and his people north to Khalkha. Dorgon sent an army, but Tenggis’s Khalkha allies shielded him; he returned to Beijing in 1648 and the court let him go unpunished [1]. Jagchid calls this the first Mongol rebellion against Manchu rule. Mongols loyal to the Qing called the Sünid *Sünid khugaichi*, “Sünid traitors,” and in Shilingol they were mocked as *köke Sünid*, “green Sünid,” for their stubbornness, though the flight was the Left Banner’s alone. In the nationalist 1930s, Jagchid writes, the slander turned into praise for the brave Sünid [1].

## The two banners

The league had 113 *sumu* (companies of about 150 households). Sünid Left had twenty and Sünid Right thirteen, which made the Right Banner, in Jagchid’s words, a banner of medium size [1]. Even the two Sünid banners had little to do with each other, Jagchid writes, while each dealt closely with Beijing [1]. Within the league, Abaga and Sünid often joined to balance the Üjümüchin Right Banner, whose princely house supplied most league heads and deputies [1].

## 1912–1945

When the Bogd Khan’s army came south in 1913, the first large battle between Mongolian and Chinese troops was fought in Sünid Left Banner, on 20 May, with heavy losses on both sides [3]; the [Five Roads campaign](/entry/five-roads-campaign) entry has the war. In 1933, when Demchugdongrub began the autonomy movement, the Sünid Left prince Rinchinwangdud and two of his banner’s untitled princes, Magsurjab and Khorjurjab, were among the few Shilingol nobles who backed him [1]. Rinchinwangdud had become head of Shilingol League by the Second Mongolian Congress at Hohhot in October 1937, where Demchugdongrub’s son Dugursürüng sat as jasag of Sünid Right [1]. In August 1945 the Soviet and Mongolian advance reached the great monastery of Chagaan Obo Süm on the northern border of Sünid [1].

The maps also carry Chinese slips pasted over the Mongol labels. A typed library note on the 1889 map (cat. 822) explains them: Chinese translations of the place names were added to show how the Lifanyuan, the Qing board for Mongol affairs, processed each banner’s map [5]. On that map each slip gives a feature’s direction and distance in *li* from the seat of the Left or the Right Banner. One of them marks a mountain as the seat of the Right Banner’s prince, and another places the spring Jurgatu (珠爾噶圖泉) 80 *li* north of the Left Banner [5]. The 1907 map of Sünid Left (cat. 824) is drawn on a grid. Its slips name the banner’s ruling prince, Mani…rjab (part of the name is illegible), and mark the prince’s pasture beside Bayan mountain, temples, lakes, a main road and a telegraph line [5]. That telegraph line was probably the one Russia ran from Kyakhta to Kalgan from 1897 [2]. A slip on the diagonal north-eastern edge of the 1907 map reads “pasture cut from Dariganga and granted in Qianlong 4,” that is, in 1739 [5]. The sources consulted do not otherwise record this grant.

Charleux notes that on the maps of Sünid Left, nine mountains are called *oboγa* (ovoo) [4, n. 35], and reproduces details of the 1901 Right Banner map showing a mountain ovoo beside a mountain that marks the boundary, and boundary ovoos linked by a boundary line [4, figs 5d and 12c]. Most Inner Mongolian banner maps ignore the Qing rule that maps be drawn on a measuring grid; the 1889 map of both Sünid banners (cat. 822) is one of eight Shilingol maps that follow it [4, n. 26]. The [Shilingol League](/entry/shilingol-league) entry describes the collection as a whole.

## Open questions

- **Submission dates.** 1637 (Atwood [2]) or 1638–1642 banner by banner (Jagchid [1]).
- **The 1739 grant from Dariganga.** Known so far only from the slip on cat. 824 [5].
- **Reading the maps.** The place names are in Mongol script, Manchu and Chinese. Haltod’s *Mongolische Ortsnamen aus mongolischen Manuskriptkarten* (1966) lists them and would let the banner seat, temples and ovoos be placed on the atlas map.
