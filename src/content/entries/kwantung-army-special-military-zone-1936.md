In March 1936 the Kwantung Army and the Manchukuo State Railways issued a printed sheet headed 海拉爾附近軍事特別地域地圖, “Map of the special military zone near Hailar.” It carries a map and four notices, set in Japanese, Chinese, Russian and English. It is the only primary document on this zone that the atlas holds.

## What the notices say

Two orders are printed under the map.

- **The Kwantung Army commander** (dated Shōwa 11, March, with no day given) forbids anyone without his permit to inspect the dispositions of troops in the areas “circumscribed … by black lines,” to survey, photograph, sketch or model the land and coasts there, or to carry out works or handle explosives and inflammable materials in a way the local Japanese military authorities have prohibited. A second article forbids flying over the area marked by the black dotted line. Damaging or moving the marker posts, stones and boards set up under the order is also punishable.
- **The head of the Manchukuo State Railways** (dated Kangde 3, 1 April, which is 1936) tells passengers to keep to the commander’s order and forbids them to look out of the coaches between **Hake** and **Ugunor** stations.

The English text is full of errors, and the Russian and Chinese versions follow the Japanese.

## What the map draws

The map shows the railway across Hulunbuir with its stations (Zabaikalsk on the Soviet side, then Manzhouli, Zalainor, Hailar, Ugunor, Hake and others), Hulun Nur and Buir Nur, and the Soviet Union to the north and Outer Mongolia to the south. A dealer’s listing gives its scale as 1:2,750,000; the atlas has not checked that, but fitting the sheet to four places gives errors of 0.3 to 7 km, so it is close to a measured map.

- **Horizontal hatching** fills a wide zone along the frontier, bounded by a heavy dashed line. By the notice, this is the area closed to aircraft.
- **Cross-hatching** marks four blocks inside it: around Hailar, around Manzhouli and Zalainor, in the north-east toward the Argun and Amur, and around Buir Nur. These are the areas within the black lines.
- The Hailar block lies between Ugunor and Hake, the two stations the railway notice names. The notice does not mention the other three blocks.

The Timeline Map draws the zone and the four blocks for 1936 from the sheet. The outlines were traced from the printed hatching and are approximate, up to about 10 km off the printed line.

Where the sheet’s hatching ran beyond the Manchukuo frontier into Outer Mongolia (along the south edge near 47.3° N, and at a salient near 119.5° E), the atlas cuts it to the frontier drawn on a 1935 Osaka Mainichi map of Manchuria, Korea and Japan. That map draws the Manchukuo line along the Khalkhin Gol, so the strip around Nomonhan, which Mongolia also claimed, stays inside the zone. The cut removes about 1,900 km² from the zone and about 700 km² from the restricted blocks.

## What is not known

- The sheet is dated March 1936 and gives no end date. Nothing consulted says how long the zone applied, so the map shows it for 1936 only.
- No source consulted says why these limits were chosen. The Japanese army was building the Hailar fortress at the time (below), and the zone covers that frontier, but the sheet itself does not say so.
- The dealer’s listing says the sheet is probably unique and that no other copy was found. That is the dealer’s statement and has not been checked.

## Context: the Hailar fortress

Japanese Wikipedia dates the building of the Hailar fortress (海拉爾要塞) from June 1934 to 1937. It describes five defensive positions around Hailar, mainly garrisoned by the Kwantung Army’s 8th Border Garrison Unit, and a fortress organisation formed in February 1938 and disbanded in 1944. The Soviet attack on 9 August 1945 reached it, and the garrison surrendered on 21 August. The article does not mention the special military zone. Whether the zone was tied to the fortress is the atlas’s inference, not something these sources say.
