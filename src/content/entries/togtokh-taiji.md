Togtokh Taiji (1863–1922), also written Togtakhu, was a minor nobleman (*taiji*) of the Front Gorlos Banner in Jirim League, in the far east of Inner Mongolia [1]. When the late Qing opened his banner to Chinese settlement he led an armed rising against it, and after Mongolia declared independence he went north with his men and served the new state [1, 2, 3].

*A note on sources.* The entry rests on Christopher Atwood’s encyclopedia [1] and *Young Mongols and Vigilantes* [3], [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid)’s memoir [2] and Tachibana Makoto’s archive-based study [4]. The fullest biography in English, G. Navaangnamjil’s, has not been consulted (see the open questions).

## The rising against colonization (1906–1911)

Chinese farmers had settled Front Gorlos since the eighteenth century, and its Mongols had slowly become farmers themselves. From 1902 the Qing “New Policies” brought massive Chinese colonization to the banner, and in 1910 its collective land system was officially dissolved [1]. Togtokh rose in 1906 [1]. His band, armed with Russian and Japanese rifles, killed government surveyors and soldiers and looted Chinese shops [1]. Jagchid calls his the best organized of the “Mongolian bandit” groups of the last Qing years, and says it attacked Chinese-farmed districts across Mukden, Kirin and Heilongjiang provinces [2]. The rising was suppressed, and Togtokh found asylum with his band in Russia [1].

## In independent Mongolia (1911–1922)

When Mongolia declared independence, Jagchid writes, Togtokh and his followers went to Urga and joined the new Mongolian forces with enthusiasm [2]. Atwood describes him as having fought his way into Khalkha and become an honoured figure in the theocratic government [3]. In August 1912 he and Prince Utai of Khorchin attacked Chinese towns in Jirim League; both attacks failed [1].

In January 1913 the Mongolian war ministry proposed sending him with Chimidtseren along the Yeegüzer Khutagt monastery route of the [Five Roads campaign](/entry/five-roads-campaign). The Bogd Khan held him back and ordered his troops sent under Naidanjav instead [4]. The sources consulted give no reason.

He adopted the orphaned boy Buyannemekhü, born in 1902 in a Gobi banner of Khalkha, at about the age of ten. In Togtokh’s household the boy was taught Mongolian, Manchu and some Chinese, listened to the minstrels who performed for Togtokh, and picked up an East Mongolian accent. Buyannemekhü became Mongolia’s first revolutionary poet, and his “Mongolian Internationale” served for years as the country’s anthem [1, 3].

## Open questions

- **From Russia to Urga.** Atwood says he found asylum in Russia [1]; Jagchid and Atwood’s later book have him going to Urga after independence [2, 3]. How and when he moved is not given in the sources consulted.
- **Why the Bogd Khan held him back in 1913** [4].
- **The fuller biography.** G. Navaangnamjil, “A Brief Biography of the Determined Hero Togtokh,” in *Mongolian Heroes of the Twentieth Century*, trans. Urgunge Onon (New York: AMS Press, 1976), 43–76, is the next source to get.
