The Five Roads campaign (Таван замын байлдаан, “the battle of the five roads”) was the Bogd Khanate's attempt in 1913 to drive the forces of the Republic of China out of Inner Mongolia and join it to the new Mongolian state. Mongolian historiography uses that name; Inner Mongolian historiography calls it “Үхэр жилийн үймээн” (the Ox Year Disturbance), and Chinese historiography treats it as an incursion by Outer Mongolian rebels (Myagmarsambuu).

## Background

After the Bogd Khanate declared independence in December 1911, the Barga and many Inner Mongols joined it (Myagmarsambuu). In late 1912 the government sent a delegation under the foreign minister Khanddorj to St Petersburg to buy or borrow arms in quantity, meaning to issue them in Khalkha, Khovd and Hulunbuir; Russia first agreed and then refused, saying it would itself guard Outer Mongolia (Baabar, citing Tachibana). The government's appeal to the people of Inner Mongolia to join in driving out the Chinese dates from early 1913 (Baabar).

[Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid) describes how the leagues bordering Outer Mongolia answered Urga's earlier circular, issued in the Bogd Khan's name, inviting Inner Mongolian nobles, officials and commoners to join the independence struggle. Hulunbuir joined at once. The Ulanchab nobles were "comparatively pro-Ulaanbaatar" but, under pressure from Beijing, only expressed sympathy and did not declare independence. In Shilingol only the league head, Yangsang, opposed the movement; Outer Mongolian troops took him into custody at his residence in Abaga Left Banner and imprisoned him in Urga, after which the deputy head, Sodnamrabdan of Üjümüchin Right Banner, acted for the league with a "wait-and-see" policy (Jagchid, pp. 13–14). Atwood counts 49 Inner Mongolian banners and says 35 of them expressed some support for Urga. Prince Udai of Khorchin Right Front Banner attacked Chinese towns in Jirim on 20 August 1912 and was back in retreat by 12 September, and Togtakhu Taiji's attack was also defeated (Atwood, “Sino-Mongolian War”). On 23 January 1913, Atwood writes, the Mongolian government ordered the Shilingol and Ulaanchab leagues to mobilize 2,000 troops; Baabar puts the government's decision at 25 January. The date difference is small and may reflect an order against a decision, but neither source says so. Jagchid does not date the arrest. Tachibana, working from the Bogd Khan government’s correspondence with the Shilingol banners, dates it to 27 October 1912, when troops under Navaangombo came to Yangsang’s residence, exchanged fire with his armed men, seized him and his seals, and took him to Urga on the charge of having “resisted the Mongol government” (Tachibana 2005). Yangsang had refused to pass on Urga’s letters to the other leagues and was reported to be dealing with the Chinese. At the time of his arrest only the two Khuuchid banners of Shilingol had declared for the Bogd Khan. On 13 November he declared his own allegiance and was kept on as league head with a new seal bearing the Soyombo in place of the Qing seal, and Urga’s officials warned the other banners to report their submission to the army camp at Morotsog Süm in Dariganga, or be arrested as he had been. By January 1913 all ten Shilingol banners had declared for the Bogd Khan, and in April 1913 Urga made the vice head, Sodnamrabdan, general of the league’s troops, a post that Khalkha had and Inner Mongolia had never had (Tachibana 2005). Tachibana warns against the familiar figure of 35 of 49 banners: it goes back to Magsarjav’s history of 1927, other scholars count at least 36 or 38, and in Shilingol at least the declarations followed pressure and an arrest (Tachibana 2005). Beijing, for its part, tried to hold the Inner Mongolian princes with the Regulations for the Treatment of Mongolia (1912), which kept their titles, ranks and banner powers intact (Jagchid, p. 14).

## The five routes

Baabar names the routes as: Чуулалт хаалга (Kalgan) and the western part; Бат хаалга, in Darkhan Muumingan banner (Bailingmiao, this atlas's [Bat-khaalag](/entry/bailingmiao)); Долоннуур (Dolonnor); Хөх хот ([Hohhot](/entry/hohhot)); and Бугат (Baotou), along the Хатан гол (the Yellow River). About 10,000 men took part, in both accounts.

The two sources organise the command differently:

- **Mongoltoli (Myagmarsambuu)** describes two main thrusts. Forces under Sonomdorj and Chagdarjav advanced from the southern edge of Tüsheet Khan aimag toward Ulaanchab and Hohhot; forces under Manlai Baatar Damdinsüren and Yalguun Baatar Sumiya advanced from Dariganga and the Egüzer monastery toward Shilingol, Juu Uda, Chahar and Dolonnor.
- **Baabar** names Damdinsüren as overall commander, with gün Chimidtseren on the Egüzer monastery route, Khaisan and the deputy minister Bavuujav on the Dariganga route, Nasan-Arvijikh on the route toward the Sünid and Dörvöd Wang banners, and Sumiya on the Yellow River route.

Jagchid names a different pair of commanders for the eastern thrust: Urga, he writes, sent Shirun *jiangjun* (general) and Namsarai *bagatur* (hero) with an army to take Doloonnor, the religious center between the eastern Shilingol and Chahar leagues, to intimidate the Inner Mongolian leagues and banners into joining the new state (Jagchid, p. 15). Neither name appears in Baabar's or Mongoltoli's account; whether they are the same men under other names is not established here.

The map draws the five routes as arrows. They show directions of advance only: neither source gives a front line or names the places held, and the starting points are approximate (see the map's campaign note).

## Course and outcome

The sources agree on the outline: the army advanced in the spring, fought for some months, and was withdrawn late in the year under Russian pressure. They differ on the details.

| Point | Baabar | Mongoltoli (Myagmarsambuu) |
|---|---|---|
| Decision and start | Government decided on 25 January 1913; decrees around 1 February; army left Khüree in February | Troops entered Inner Mongolia at the end of the “middle spring month” of 1913 (a traditional month name, so March or April) |
| Command | Da Lama Puntsagdorj and Damdinsüren named commanders; a July 1913 decree set up a special office under the Egüzer Khutagt Galsandash | Damdinsüren appointed commander of the southern forces on 1 July 1913 |
| Fighting | Mostly small engagements, mostly won by the Mongols; an attack on Hohhot on 7 July 1913 with over a thousand men was beaten, and the Mongols fell back to “Вангийн сүм” (a prince's temple, not yet identified) | By the last month of autumn 1913 the army had “liberated most of Inner Mongolia” |
| End | In December 1913 the government promised Russia to withdraw | A Bogd Khan decree halted the fighting and withdrew the troops in November 1913 |
| Chinese forces | Larger and better armed (no figure) | 30,000–40,000, as estimated by researchers |
| Judgement | The victories were real but naive: Russia let the war run only to press China before the three-way talks, then stopped it | A major war that showed Beijing Mongolia could not be taken by force and brought it to negotiate |

Jagchid's account differs on how the eastern thrust ended. In his telling the force sent against Doloonnor had to withdraw "because of heavy pressure from Chinese troops" before it could take the town, and once it had gone the Chinese occupied Doloonnor and killed the Kanjurwa Khutugtu, a lama sympathetic to uniting the two Mongolias, a killing that "created a great disturbance among the Inner Mongols" (Jagchid, p. 15). Baabar and Atwood put the end of the campaign down to Russian pressure; Jagchid, for this front, to Chinese military pressure. The two need not exclude each other, but the sources are set side by side here rather than reconciled. Atwood records the campaign's furthest point here as "the outskirts of Dolonnuur," which agrees with Jagchid that the town itself was not taken.

Baabar also relays, from the Mongolian translation of Sven Hedin's travel account, a count of about fifty clashes, all lost by the Chinese. Hedin was not a witness to the fighting he describes, and the figure is his. Baabar adds that [Frans August Larson](/entry/frans-august-larson), asked by Yuan Shikai to mediate, went to Khüree but failed to persuade the Bogd Khan to stop (citing Larson's memoir).

After the withdrawal, Baabar writes, the Mongol forces turned on each other: Khalkha troops under Magsarjav were sent against Babuujav, whom he calls a Kharchin gün. The 1915 Kyakhta agreement then made only Outer Mongolia autonomous, leaving Inner Mongolia to China.

## Who fought, and on which side

The army was not purely Mongolian state troops. Atwood's *Young Mongols and Vigilantes in Inner Mongolia's Interregnum Decades* says that in the 1913–1914 conflict the Mongolian armies reached the passes of the Yinshan north of Baotou and Guisui, recruiting local banner militia, garrisons and bandits, "perhaps about half Mongol and half Chinese" (p. 558). The bandit bands, called *duli*, were a product of this war as much as a cause of it: the war released guns and demobilized soldiers into the countryside, which fed banditry in the following years (p. 205). Atwood adds that the troops were abandoned when the Kyakhta treaty ended the invasion, and that some became local militia and others bandits (p. 558). In Atwood's *Encyclopedia* the same point appears: locally recruited soldiers were disbanded with their rifles, and Babujab refused the treaty and disturbed the frontier until 1917 (“Sino-Mongolian War”). Baabar's report that Khalkha troops under Magsarjav were sent against Babuujav (below) belongs to the same aftermath; he gives Babuujav as a Kharchin gün, Atwood as a Monggoljin bandit leader, and this atlas does not decide between them.

Inner Mongols also stood on the Chinese side. Atwood names Adjutant Yue Shan of Kheshigten, who opposed Khalkha independence; he and his son Yue Jingtao (Murungga, 1880/85 to about 1940) sided with the Chinese against the invading armies, and Yue Shan was elected to the first parliament in 1913 for the Kuomintang (*Young Mongols and Vigilantes*, pp. 91–92). Duke Nasun'arbijikhu of Khorchin Left Rear Banner deserted to the Chinese in the middle of the campaign (“Sino-Mongolian War”). In the Hetao, the lama Wangdanima (Bandida Gegen), who was agitating for union with Mongolia, was ambushed by the Suiyuan *dutong* in 1913, deported to Beijing and pardoned by Yuan Shikai (*Young Mongols and Vigilantes*, p. 217).

## Larson's account of the war

[Frans August Larson](/entry/frans-august-larson) served in Peking as the Mongolian representative and adviser to Yuan Shikai. His memoir, *Larson, Duke of Mongolia* (Boston: Little, Brown and Company, 1930, pp. 223–236), is therefore a participant's account, and is set out here as such. He writes that the war began on the border at once in Ulanchab, Chahar, Silingol and northern Juu Uda (p. 223 ff.). He reports that a lama commander he calls "Muronga" killed an Englishman, Mr. Grant of the Chinese Telegraph Service, and some Chinese travellers, and that he was arrested and "shaken to death" in a felt on a camel before he reached Urga (pp. 226–227). Atwood's Murungga, Yue Jingtao, lived to about 1940 and sided with China, so the two are evidently not the same man; the identification of Larson's lama is not made by any source consulted here.

Asked by Yuan Shikai to mediate, Larson went first to Urga, where he could not get the order to stop, and then to the fighters' headquarters in a temple (pp. 227–233). There, he writes, the leader of the border fighters, whom he calls "Prince Na," agreed to halt his men and go to Peking with 200 of them as Yuan Shikai's bodyguard, and Larson escorted him through Kalgan (pp. 228–234). Baabar's Nasan-Arvijikh and Atwood's Duke Nasun'arbijikhu are similar names, but no source states that either is Larson's "Prince Na," and Atwood has the Duke deserting rather than being taken to Peking. Larson also dates the Russo-Mongolian agreement to 3 November 1912 and the Russo-Chinese agreement to 5 November 1913 (p. 235).

## The Russian ending

Atwood dates the Russo-Chinese declaration to 5 November 1913 (23 October, Old Style). It followed the Mongolian occupation, "with Russian assistance," of substantial areas of Inner Mongolia and defined Outer Mongolia as autonomous under Chinese suzerainty (“Kyakhta Trilateral Treaty”). Despite the October mission of Namnansürüng to St Petersburg, Russia telegraphed on 28 October ordering the campaign stopped; cold and shortages forced the Khalkha and Barga troops to withdraw, and the withdrawal was formalized by the Kyakhta treaty, whose conference opened on 8 September 1914 and which was signed on 7 June 1915 (“Sino-Mongolian War”; “Kyakhta Trilateral Treaty”). Outer Mongolia kept Dariganga but lost Inner Mongolia and Hulunbuir. After 1915, Atwood says, some New Barga bannermen and Chakhar soldiers were resettled in Mongolia as Damdinsürüng's subjects. The campaign left Mongolia in debt to Russia: 332,000 gold rubles by 1916, more than a fifth of central government expenditure (“Sino-Mongolian War”).

Baabar dates the government's promise to Russia to withdraw to December 1913 and Mongoltoli puts the Bogd Khan's halting decree in November 1913; Atwood's 28 October telegram precedes both, which suggests the order was given by Russia first and formalized by Mongolia afterwards. That is an inference from the dates, not a statement in any source.

Jagchid, writing about 1935, later called a "bandit" ambush an exact copy of the 1913 killing of the Kanjurwa Khutugtu "by an army force disguised as a bandit group" (p. 107). That is a retrospective judgment by an Inner Mongolian participant and is not corroborated in the other sources used here.
