The Üjümüchin (Ujimqin, Wuzhumuqin) are a Mongol people of Shilingol (Xilingol) League, and until 1949 they formed two banners, Üjümüchin Left and Üjümüchin Right, in the league’s north-east corner, where Shilingol meets Outer Mongolia and Hulunbuir. Jagchid calls Shilingol the league with the best pastures, with Üjümüchin in the east and Sünid in the west “especially famous for their excellent horses” (Jagchid, p. 2). Atwood describes the Üjümüchin as known for the embroidered hems of their robes (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, “Üjümüchin,” p. 565).

## Origins and the Qing banners

Atwood writes that Üjümüchin was a camp district of the Chakhar tümen in the sixteenth century, and that its princes, like those of Khuuchid and Sünid, descended from Bodi Alag Khan (1519–47), grandson of Dayan Khan (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 565). In 1627 the princes of Sünid, Üjümüchin and Abaga revolted against Ligdan Khan and moved north-west into Khalkha (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, “Ligdan Khan,” p. 334).

The sources differ on when they submitted to the Qing:

- **1637.** Atwood has the Üjümüchin nobles surrendering to the Qing in 1637, after Ligdan’s defeat, and two of them being made jasags (rulers) of the Right and Left Banners (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 565, and “Shiliin Gol,” p. 501).
- **1641.** Jagchid writes that the Üjümüchin tribe submitted to the Manchus in 1641 and was divided into two banners, the ruler of the Right Banner receiving the title of first-ranking prince, Khoshoi Sechin Chin Wang (Jagchid, p. 1).

The header shows both years. The two banners were among the ten that made up Shilingol League (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 501). According to Jagchid, most of the league’s heads and deputy heads came from the princely house of Üjümüchin Right, and Abaga and Sünid often combined to balance its weight within the league (Jagchid, p. 6 n.).

## Under the Republic

Atwood writes that the Shilingol banners “were forced into submission to the Republic of China in 1915” and that Üjümüchin “remained isolated despite incorporation into the Republic of China after 1915” (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, pp. 501, 565). In 1912–1913 they did declare for the Bogd Khanate. After Outer Mongolian troops arrested the league head Yangsang in October 1912, Urga ordered the banners that had not yet submitted to report to its army camp in Dariganga or be arrested in their turn; the Üjümüchin Left Banner sent its submission in December 1912, and by January 1913 all ten Shilingol banners had declared. In April 1913 Urga made Sodnamrabdan of Üjümüchin Right general of the league’s troops (Tachibana 2005). The atlas has found no source saying the banners joined the Mongolian People’s Republic after 1924. During the [Babujab](/entry/babujab) rising of 1916, Jagchid writes, Üjümüchin and the other eastern Shilingol banners were in turmoil while the west of the league was untouched (Jagchid, p. 17).

**Prince Sodnamrabdan.** In 1924, when the old league head Yangsang retired, the customary successor was his deputy, Sodnamrabdan, prince of Üjümüchin Right. Yangsang wanted [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub) of Sünid Right instead. Jagchid, whose father Lobsangchoijur was [Gungsangnorbu](/entry/gungsangnorbu)’s representative and a friend of Sodnamrabdan, writes that his father brokered a compromise: Sodnamrabdan became league head and Demchugdongrub his deputy, and Demchugdongrub afterwards addressed the older prince as *bagshi*, “teacher” (Jagchid, pp. 23–24). Jagchid calls Sodnamrabdan “the richest person in all of Inner Mongolia.” When the Panchen Lama toured Inner Mongolia, Sodnamrabdan invited him to Üjümüchin Right, where the lama gave the Kalachakra initiation; Sodnamrabdan declined to build the lama a temple there because his family had long been the main patrons of the Dalai Lama (Jagchid, pp. 42–43).

## The Japanese approach, 1933–1935

After the Kwantung Army took Rehe in 1933, Jagchid writes, it sent Major Tanaka Hisashi and AltanOchir (Jin Yongchang) to Üjümüchin to win over Sodnamrabdan, then head of the league. He received them coldly and refused them a Special Service Office; the army set one up near the banner office anyway, but the banner gave its staff under Morishima only two yurts and no supplies, and by winter they had to leave (Jagchid, pp. 57–58). The banner had to accept Tanaka’s intelligence office the following spring (Jagchid, p. 66).

Jagchid gives the banners’ position as the reason for Sodnamrabdan’s difficulty: his banner and the other eastern banners of the league had to buy grain and other farm produce from Manchukuo-held Rehe (Jagchid, p. 57). That dependence did not make them part of Manchukuo. When Demchugdongrub visited Üjümüchin during the Panchen Lama’s stay there and proposed uniting all the leagues and banners to face the Japanese, Sodnamrabdan agreed, with his senior official Togtakhu, who ran the banner for the ailing prince (Jagchid, p. 66). Togtakhu and Duke Dobdan of Üjümüchin attended the conference for autonomy at Bailingmiao that opened on 9 October 1933 (Jagchid, p. 74).

In early summer 1935 Itagaki Seishirō, deputy chief of staff of the Kwantung Army, came to Üjümüchin with other staff officers and AltanOchir as interpreter. Sodnamrabdan pleaded poor health and had Itagaki talk directly to Demchugdongrub, the first meeting between the Inner Mongolian leaders and senior Japanese officers. Itagaki offered support for Mongol independence but said he could not discuss uniting eastern and western Inner Mongolia, because that touched Manchukuo’s sovereignty (Jagchid, pp. 127–128). According to Atwood, the first Mongolian Congress of April 1936 was held in Üjümüchin (Atwood, *Young Mongols and Vigilantes*, p. 1010).

## Under Mengjiang

Jagchid describes a crisis in Üjümüchin Left Banner around 1938. Its young jasag Dorji, who took over from the regent Duke Dobdan at eighteen, was accused of raising taxes and mistreating his people. The abbot Lubsang, known as the Kabji Lama, backed by the Japanese Special Service Office at Nunai Süme, led the banner people in refusing taxes and set up a school and a workshop at his monastery. Prince Sungjingwangchug was sent to settle it, banned the lama’s projects and forgave the protesters. Jagchid adds that some of Dorji’s followers tried to get help from Outer Mongolia and the Soviet Union (Jagchid, pp. 234–235).

## 1945 and after

After the Soviet–Mongolian invasion of August 1945, Atwood writes, the young jasag of Üjümüchin Left Banner, Minjurdorji, led 1,785 Üjümüchins to migrate to Mongolia (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 565). Whether he is the Dorji of Jagchid’s account is likely but not stated by either source. Khökhbaatar counts the herders of Üjümüchin Left and Khuuchid Right banners among the largest group migrations of 1945, and notes that Chinese Communist reports of the time described part of the movement as people “driven off” by Outer Mongolian troops (Khökhbaatar). In 1989 the Üjümüchin of Mongolia numbered 2,100 and lived in Sergelen sum of Eastern Province (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 565).

In 1949 the Üjümüchin and Khuuchid banners were merged; the Xilingol Party history office calls the result, in 1950, the Eastern United Banner (东部联合旗) (Xilingol Party history office, “草原记忆（八）”); in 1956 the combined territory was divided again into two Üjümüchin banners, East and West Ujimqin, which had a combined population of 127,700 in 1990, 83,700 of them Mongols (Atwood, *Encyclopedia*, p. 565).

Charleux lists three of them (cats. 807, 809 and 810) among the few Inner Mongolian banner maps drawn on a grid (Charleux 2021, n. 26), though the online scan of cat. 810 shows a ruled frame and no grid. Caroline Humphrey reads cat. 810, the 1890 map of the Right Banner, closely. It has no grid and no compass points. Twelve inscriptions around the margin, each facing inward, name the neighbouring lands in twelve directions, and they show that south is at the top. Rivers and lakes are seen from above and mountains from the side, half of them drawn one way up and half the other, as a traveller moving down the Balur river would meet them. A sealed inscription in the centre reads, in Humphrey’s translation, that the ruling prince, Altanhuyagt, had lived and nomadized on both sides of the Balur for several generations. Humphrey points out that the prince is drawn with his back to the emperor in the south (Humphrey 2021, illustration 3). On the 1901 map of the Left Banner (cat. 807) the banner’s eastern corner appears as a great forested mountain, Soyolji; Charleux shows that this was an imperial hunting ground outside the banner, ringed by twelve red circles marked *obo* and eight marked *qaraγul*, guard post, and sets it beside the same corner on the 1907 map (cat. 809) (Charleux 2021, fig. 17). The 1907 Left Banner map shows about eighteen mountains with a tuft on top, the other two far fewer, and on the maps of the Right Banner eleven mountains are called *oboγa* (Charleux 2021, nn. 35 and 123). The [Shilingol League](/entry/shilingol-league) entry describes the collection.

## On the Timeline Map

The map draws the Üjümüchin banners inside Shilingol League in every year, reaching north-east to the corner where Shilingol, Outer Mongolia and Hulunbuir meet. Their frontier with Outer Mongolia follows the Nakamura map of 1941, which agrees within about 10–30 km with Atwood’s map of the Qing leagues (borders as in 1820) and with the 1962 Sino-Mongolian treaty line. A 1930s English map of the banners, used for the frontier elsewhere, runs the line 30–80 km further south and would put northern Üjümüchin in Mongolia; the atlas does not follow it here. The same three maps put the whole of Üjümüchin outside Manchukuo, so the map does too, against the Narangoa and Cribb atlas, which puts a band of eastern Shilingol in Manchukuo. The banners’ seats are not yet placed.
