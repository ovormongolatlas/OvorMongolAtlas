Saichungga (1914–1973) was a Chakhar Mongol poet and essayist, one of the founders of modern Inner Mongolian literature [1]. Educated in Japan, he came home to found a girls’ school and edit textbooks in [Mengjiang](/entry/mengjiang), and in 1945 he served for four months as [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub)’s secretary for cultural affairs. After the Soviet-Mongolian invasion that August he backed union with the Mongolian People’s Republic, studied in Ulaanbaatar, and became the Chinese Communist Party’s leading Mongol poet in Inner Mongolia. He died in Shanghai in 1973 after persecution and exile.

*A note on sources.* The entry rests on Christopher Atwood’s 1999 study [1], the fullest scholarly account, and on his later encyclopedia entry [3], which adds his life after 1947. Uchida Takashi’s study of the Mongol students’ journal in Japan [4] covers his years in Tokyo. Atwood’s English translations of the 1945 poems and of the essay “A Ghost” are in copyright, so they are paraphrased here. Spellings of his name vary: Sayičungγa, Na. Sayinčoγtu and Sainchogtu are all in use.

## Education (1914–1941)

Saichungga was born on 23 March 1914 into the herding family of Nasundelger and his wife Degjima, in the No. 2 sumu of the Plain-Blue Banner of Chakhar, in today’s Xulun Hoh banner [1, 3]. Relatives taught him to read and introduced him to Mongolian oral poetry. He went to primary school in his banner from 1929 to 1933 and worked as a banner clerk [1, 3]. In 1936 the Japanese Good Neighbor Society (Zenrin Kyōkai) chose him for a Japanese-run school in Chakhar, and then for study in Japan [3].

He went to Japan in the spring of 1937, at twenty-three, as a student sent by the Mengjiang government. After a year of Japanese he studied education in the ethics-education course of Tōyō University’s vocational school from April 1938 to December 1941 [4]; Atwood calls it the Teacher’s College of Tokyo Oriental University [3]. In Tokyo he was one of the editors of *Shine Mongol* (“New Mongolia”), the Mongol students’ journal. Its second issue carries two of his poems, “My Youth” and “Let Us Rise and Strive,” and his Mongolian translations of Kitahara Hakushū and Mushanokōji Saneatsu; he also translated part of Yano Jin’ichi’s history of modern Mongolia for it [4]. In June 1941 he met the linguist Hattori Shirō and helped him translate the navy issue of the Mongolian edition of *FRONT*, the Japanese army’s propaganda magazine [4]. That places him in the generation of Japan-educated Inner Mongolians the atlas follows elsewhere, among them [Khafungga](/entry/khafungga) and [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid), whom the period’s competing powers all tried to win.

## Teacher, editor and official (1941–1945)

On his return, in Atwood’s 1999 account, he founded what Atwood calls western Inner Mongolia’s first formal girls’ school, the “West Sönid Certified Home Economist Girls’ School,” in the home region of the [West Sünid ruling house](/entry/west-sunid-ruling-house), Demchugdongrub’s own banner. He also helped edit textbooks with the “Develop Mongolia” organization in [Kalgan](/entry/kalgan) (Zhangjiakou) [1]. Atwood’s later encyclopedia entry says only that he taught at the girls’ school, which Demchugdongrub sponsored [3].

Much of his writing appeared in *Manduqu Mongghol* (“Mongolia Rising”), the [Mongolian Autonomous State](/entry/mongolian-autonomous-state)’s main journal of opinion and literature, published from Kalgan and edited by [Gombojab Hangin](/entry/gombojab-hangin) [1]. A community-memorial source used in Hangin’s entry says the two men were brothers; see Open questions below.

In 1945 he served for four months as secretary for cultural affairs to Demchugdongrub, then president of the Mongolian Autonomous State [1, 4]. The post made him, briefly, an official of the regime.

## Writings

Atwood lists a compact but varied body of published work [1]:

- *The Heart’s Companion*, poems, written in Tokyo in 1939 and published in 1941.
- *Sand Dune Motherland* (*Elesü mangqan-u eke nutuγ*), the diary of a summer vacation in 1940; Uchida records a copy printed by the Chairman’s Office press in November 1941 [4], and Atwood’s encyclopedia dates it 1942 [3]. Its thinly veiled criticism of identifiable people caused controversy, though eastern Mongols read it as praise of pastoral life.
- *Light of the Heart*, 1942.
- *Notes on Home Improvement*, October 1942, addressed to Mongolian women, on households, education, science and hygiene, in the stated service of building “a new Mongolia.”
- *The Rising Song of Our Mongolia*, letters and essays, April 1944 (1945 in Atwood’s encyclopedia [3]).

The journal’s planned second volume never appeared, cut off by the Soviet-Mongolian invasion of August 1945. Atwood recovered the 1945 works, an essay and eight poems, from the surviving printer’s sheets of that volume and published them for the first time; much of his analysis turns on them [1].

### Chinggis Khan

Konagaya Yuki’s 2012 study of the modern origins of Chinggis Khan worship adds a further thread [2]. *The Heart’s Companion*, she reports, contains a poem titled “Chinggis’s Descendants” (チンギスの子孫), thought to date from about 1940, and Saichungga studied Chinggis Khan while in Japan. She also cites a diary entry of 22 August 1940 in which he records humming a “Chinggis Khan military song” (成吉思汗軍歌). That citation runs from Konagaya to Professor Choiraljav of Inner Mongolia University, to a 1999 collected volume of Saichungga’s diaries, *Na. Sainčoγtu-yin Büren Zohkiol* (p. 202), and so to the diary itself [2]. Together these details put Chinggis Khan beside the reformist and pastoral themes Atwood documents, part of the atlas’s wider thread on how Chinggis Khan’s image was spread and used under Japanese sponsorship. The atlas has read Konagaya only through an OCR and translation pass over the Japanese original and has not checked her sources, so these details are weighted below Atwood.

## After 1945

After the Soviet-Mongolian invasion of August 1945, Saichungga endorsed Marshal Choibalsan’s aim of uniting Inner Mongolia with the Mongolian People’s Republic and was appointed Chakhar’s Deputy Head for Mongolian Banner Affairs. In November 1945 he went to Ulaanbaatar to study at the Sühbaatar Party Cadres Institute, where, as he absorbed the history of the Communist Party, his poetry turned strongly political [1, 3].

He returned to Inner Mongolia in November 1947, in the middle of the civil war, and took the name Na. Sainchogtu. He worked in turn for the *Inner Mongolian People’s Daily*, the propaganda committee of the Communist Party’s Inner Mongolian branch, and the secretariat of the China Writers’ Union, and became in effect the party’s poet laureate of Inner Mongolia [1, 3]. He was sent to the countryside for re-education in 1958–60 and joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1959. He published steadily until 1966 and was acknowledged as Inner Mongolia’s leading author: the poem *Blue Silk Robe* (1954), praising new freedoms for women; the collection *Our Powerful Song* (1955); his only novel, *The Spring Sun Comes from Beijing* (1956); the 1,200-line narrative poem *Nandir and Sümbür* (1957), on two young lovers during collectivization; and a poetic record of a return to his home banner (1962) [3]. In 1962 he repudiated the introspective, Buddhist-tinged romanticism of his early work [1].

In the Cultural Revolution he was attacked and tormented, partly for having studied in Japan and in the Mongolian People’s Republic, and finally exiled to Shanghai [3, 4]. He began writing poetry again in 1971 and died on 13 May 1973 [3]; Uchida gives illness as the cause [4]. Atwood cautioned in 1999 that no definitive biography of him exists [1].

His path, from Japanese-era cultural official to pan-Mongol unificationist, study in the Mongolian People’s Republic, the Communist cultural apparatus and finally persecution, resembles the course the atlas records for [Khafungga](/entry/khafungga) and, in its end, for Demchugdongrub. It is a pattern these lives share under the same pressures, not a rule to be read off from any one of them.

## His place in Inner Mongolian literature

Atwood presents Saichungga as a founder of modern Inner Mongolian literature whose cultural nationalism joined two strands that seem to pull apart: a fierce reformist commitment to modernity, science and moral renewal, and a romantic reverence for the images of Mongol nomadic life [1]. Characteristically, he announced the end of Buddhist ethics in Buddhist metaphors. His 1945 poems, in Atwood’s reading, gave voice to the Japanese-sponsored, youth-centered, ascetic and martial nationalism of late-war Inner Mongolia, a youth movement increasingly set against the older aristocratic and religious authorities [1].

Many of his poems travelled orally as songs and lost their author on the way. Of five of his poems in one Taiwan émigré folk-song collection, only two were credited to him; one was labelled an East Mongolian folk song and another a Chakhar melody [1].

## Open questions

- ***The Heart’s Companion.*** Atwood dates it to a 1939 Tokyo mimeograph published in 1941 [1, 3]; Konagaya appears to place it in 1942 [2].
- **The girls’ school.** Founded by him (Atwood 1999 [1]) or a school, sponsored by Demchugdongrub, where he taught (Atwood’s encyclopedia [3]).
- ***Sand Dune Motherland* and *The Rising Song*.** Their dates differ by a year or two across [1], [3] and [4].
- **Brother of Gombojab Hangin.** The memorial source behind Hangin’s entry names Saichungga as Hangin’s brother but puts the family in the Plain White Banner of Chakhar, where Atwood has Saichungga born in the Plain-Blue Banner. Hangin later published a study of Saichungga’s works (*Tractata Altaica*, 1976), cited in Atwood’s encyclopedia [3]; the atlas has not seen it say anything about kinship. See the [Gombojab Hangin](/entry/gombojab-hangin) entry.
- **His death.** In Shanghai, after exile, on 13 May 1973 [1, 3]; of illness, in Uchida [4].
