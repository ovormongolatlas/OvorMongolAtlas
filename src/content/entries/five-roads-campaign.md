The Five Roads campaign (Таван замын байлдаан, “the battle of the five roads”) was the war the Bogd Khan’s government in Urga fought in 1913 to hold Inner Mongolia against the Republic of China. Mongolian historiography uses that name; Inner Mongolian historiography calls it “Үхэр жилийн үймээн” (the Ox Year Disturbance), and Chinese historiography treats it as an incursion by Outer Mongolian rebels [3]. An army of seven to ten thousand men, led mostly by Inner Mongols and Barga who had come over to Urga, advanced along five routes in the spring and summer, fought the Chinese for some months, and was withdrawn late in the year when Russia refused to back it [1, 2, 5].

*A note on sources.* The fullest archival account is Tachibana Makoto’s study of the Bogd Khan government’s dealings with Shilingol League [1]. The Mongolian accounts by Baabar [2] and G. Myagmarsambuu [3] give the routes, commanders and course of the war, and Christopher Atwood’s encyclopedia [5] and *Young Mongols and Vigilantes* [6] give the outside view. [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid) [4] and [Frans August Larson](/entry/frans-august-larson) [7] write as participants. The sources differ on dates, commanders and how the war ended; the entry gives each version with its source.

## Background (1911–1912)

After the Bogd Khan’s government declared independence in December 1911, the Barga and many Inner Mongols joined it [3]. Urga sent a circular in the Bogd Khan’s name inviting the nobles, officials and commoners of Inner Mongolia to join the struggle. Hulunbuir joined at once. The Ulanchab nobles were “comparatively pro-Ulaanbaatar” but, under pressure from Beijing, only expressed sympathy [4]. Beijing tried to hold the princes with the Regulations for the Treatment of Mongolia (1912), which kept their titles, ranks and banner powers intact [4].

In August 1912 two eastern rebels armed by Urga, Prince Utai of the Khorchin Right Front Banner and [Togtokh Taiji](/entry/togtokh-taiji) of Gorlos, attacked Chinese towns in Jirim League. Utai struck on 20 August and by 12 September had retreated in disorder to Outer Mongolia; Togtokh’s attack was also defeated [5].

In Shilingol the league head, Yangsang, refused to pass on Urga’s letters to the other leagues and was reported to be dealing with the Chinese. On 27 October 1912 troops under Navaangombo came to his residence in Abaga Left Banner, exchanged fire with his armed men, seized him and his seals, and took him to Urga on the charge of having “resisted the Mongol government” [1]. Only the two Khuuchid banners of Shilingol had declared for the Bogd Khan at the time. On 13 November Yangsang declared his own allegiance and was kept on as league head with a new seal bearing the Soyombo, and Urga warned the remaining banners to report their submission to its army camp at Morotsog Süm in [Dariganga](/entry/dariganga) or be arrested as he had been [1]. By January 1913 all ten Shilingol banners had declared for the Bogd Khan [1]. Jagchid, who does not date the arrest, writes that the deputy head, Sodnamrabdan of [Üjümüchin](/entry/ujumuchin) Right Banner, then acted for the league with a “wait-and-see” policy [4].

Atwood counts 49 Inner Mongolian banners and says 35 of them expressed some support for Urga [5]. Tachibana warns against that figure: it goes back to Magsarjav’s history of 1927, other scholars count at least 36 or 38, and in Shilingol at least the declarations followed pressure and an arrest [1].

In late 1912 the government sent a delegation under the foreign minister Khanddorj to St Petersburg to buy or borrow arms; Russia first agreed and then refused, saying it would itself guard Outer Mongolia [2]. Atwood writes that Urga received a promise of Russian arms and trainers in January 1913 [5].

## The decision and the five routes (January–February 1913)

On 24 January 1913 Urga’s Interior Ministry ordered Shilingol and Ulanchab, the two leagues in which every banner had declared for the Bogd Khan, to supply 1,000 picked men each to defend their borders and key points [1]. Atwood dates the mobilization order for the 2,000 men to 23 January [5]. On 25 January the war ministry reported Chinese troops massing on the southern border and proposed sending troops along five routes [1, 2]:

| Route | Commanders (war ministry plan [1]) | Commanders (Baabar [2]) |
|---|---|---|
| Yeegüzer Khutagt monastery | Chimidtseren and Togtokh | gün Chimidtseren |
| Dariganga | [Khaisan](/entry/khaisan) and [Babujab](/entry/babujab) | Khaisan and the deputy minister Bavuujav |
| Left and Right Sünid and the Dörben Keüked banner | Nasan-Arvijikh | Nasan-Arvijikh |
| [Hohhot](/entry/hohhot) | 200 men of the Khalkha military school with the minister Sonomdorj gün | not named |
| Urad banners and the Yellow River (Baotou) | “Zutugeruto” (ズトゥゲルト in Tachibana’s rendering) | Sumiya |

The Bogd Khan approved the plan but held Togtokh back and sent his troops under Naidanjav [1]. On 1 February he appointed the Barga general [Damdinsüren](/entry/damdinsuren), the “Forefront Hero,” as overall commander [1]. Baabar names the routes after their goals, Kalgan, Bailingmiao ([Bat-khaalag](/entry/bailingmiao)), Dolonnor, Hohhot and Baotou [2]; Atwood gives the advance as toward Linxi, Dolonnor, Kalgan, Guisui (Hohhot) and the Urad banners [5].

Myagmarsambuu describes two main thrusts. Forces under Sonomdorj and Chagdarjav advanced from the southern edge of Tüsheet Khan aimag toward Ulanchab and Hohhot; forces under Damdinsüren and Yalguun Baatar Sumiya advanced from Dariganga and the Yeegüzer monastery toward Shilingol, Juu Uda, Chahar and Dolonnor [3]. Jagchid names a different pair for the eastern thrust: Urga, he writes, sent Shirun *jiangjun* (general) and Namsarai *bagatur* (hero) to take Dolonnor, the religious centre between eastern Shilingol and Chahar, to intimidate the Inner Mongolian banners into joining the new state [4].

Most of the commanders were men from Hulunbuir or Inner Mongolia who had come over to Urga. Tachibana cites the Mongolian historian Jamsran’s explanation: they knew the country, and they were fighting to free their own homelands [1]. Atwood names Babujab and Damdinsüren among them [5].

Earlier historians called the campaign a war to liberate Inner Mongolia or to widen the territory under Urga’s control. Tachibana reads the plan more narrowly. It spoke of “defence” and “interception,” and the troops went chiefly to the southern border of Khalkha and the two adjoining leagues, Shilingol and Ulanchab, whose banners had all declared for the Bogd Khan. He concludes that the deployment was largely meant to defend land Urga already counted as part of Mongolia [1].

## The fighting (spring–autumn 1913)

The army left Khüree in February [2, 5]; Myagmarsambuu has it entering Inner Mongolia at the end of the “middle spring month,” March or April [3]. In April Urga made Sodnamrabdan general of Shilingol’s troops, a post that Khalkha had and Inner Mongolia had never had [1].

The first large battle between the Mongolian and Chinese armies was fought in Sünid Left Banner on 20 May 1913, with heavy losses on both sides [1]. Baabar describes mostly small engagements, mostly won by the Mongols. An attack on Hohhot on 7 July with over a thousand men was beaten off, and the attackers fell back to “Вангийн сүм” (a prince’s temple, not identified) [2]. A decree of July 1913 set up a special office for the campaign under the Yeegüzer Khutagt Galsandash [2].

Atwood puts the army at five columns, about 7,000 to 8,000 men with rifles and five cannons; Baabar and Myagmarsambuu give about 10,000 [2, 3, 5]. At its furthest advance it held Linxi, Kheshigten Banner, Jingpeng, the outskirts of Dolonnor, Pangjiang, Erfenzi, Bailingmiao, Dashetai and Ulaan Oboo. Lack of artillery was its great weakness, and its base camps on the open steppe of Shilingol and Ulanchab had few resources; shortages of ammunition and food brought hardship and frequent plundering of the local people [5]. Myagmarsambuu writes that by the last month of autumn the army had “liberated most of Inner Mongolia,” and gives the Chinese forces as 30,000 to 40,000 men [3]. Baabar relays, from the Mongolian translation of Sven Hedin’s travel account, a count of about fifty clashes, all lost by the Chinese; Hedin did not witness the fighting [2].

On the eastern front, Jagchid writes, the force sent against Dolonnor had to withdraw “because of heavy pressure from Chinese troops” before it could take the town. The Chinese then occupied Dolonnor and killed the Kanjurwa Khutugtu, a lama sympathetic to uniting the two Mongolias, a killing that “created a great disturbance among the Inner Mongols” [4]. Writing of an ambush about 1935, Jagchid later called it an exact copy of that 1913 killing “by an army force disguised as a bandit group” [4].

## Who fought, and on which side

The soldiers were a core of Khalkha and Inner Mongolian militiamen, filled out with mixed Chinese and Mongol bandits fighting for the Russian rifles and pay Urga supplied [5]. In the Yinshan passes north of Baotou and Guisui the armies recruited banner militia, garrisons and bandits, “perhaps about half Mongol and half Chinese” [6].

Some Inner Mongols fought for China. Adjutant Yue Shan of Kheshigten opposed Khalkha independence, and he and his son Yue Jingtao (Murungga, 1880/85 to about 1940) sided with the Chinese against the invading armies; Yue Shan was elected to the first parliament in 1913 for the Kuomintang [6]. Duke Nasun’arbijikhu of Khorchin Left Rear Banner, one of the Mongolian commanders, deserted to the Chinese in the middle of the campaign [5]. In the Hetao the lama Wangdanima (Bandida Gegen), who was agitating for union with Mongolia, was ambushed by the Suiyuan *dutong* in 1913, deported to Beijing and pardoned by Yuan Shikai [6].

## Larson’s account

[Frans August Larson](/entry/frans-august-larson), who served Yuan Shikai in Peking as adviser on Mongolia, recalled the war in his memoir [7]. He writes that it broke out along the border at once in Ulanchab, Chahar, Shilingol and northern Juu Uda. A lama commander he calls “Muronga” killed an Englishman, Mr Grant of the Chinese Telegraph Service, and some Chinese travellers, and was arrested and “shaken to death” in a felt on a camel before he reached Urga [7]. Atwood’s Murungga, Yue Jingtao, sided with China and lived until about 1940, so he is a different man [6].

Asked by Yuan Shikai to mediate, Larson went to Urga, where he could not get an order to stop, and then to the fighters’ headquarters in a temple. There, he writes, their leader, “Prince Na,” agreed to halt his men and go to Peking with 200 of them as Yuan Shikai’s bodyguard, and Larson escorted him through Kalgan [7]. Baabar also reports Larson’s failure in Urga [2]. Who “Prince Na” was is uncertain (see the open questions).

## The end of the war (October 1913–1915)

The Sain Noyon Khan Namnansüren went to St Petersburg in October to seek support, but Russia refused to back the war, and on 28 October sent a telegram ordering the campaign stopped [5]. Atwood writes that this threat, the cold and the lack of supplies forced the Khalkha and Barga troops to withdraw [5]. Tachibana adds that Russia did not want Urga’s power extended into Inner Mongolia and refused help throughout, and that shortages of arms and ammunition left Urga no choice [1]. Myagmarsambuu dates the Bogd Khan’s decree halting the fighting to November 1913 [3], and Baabar dates the government’s promise to Russia to withdraw to December [2]. The Russo-Chinese declaration of 5 November 1913 (23 October, Old Style) defined Outer Mongolia as autonomous under Chinese suzerainty [5, 7].

The withdrawal was formalized by the Kyakhta treaty, negotiated from 8 September 1914 and signed on 7 June 1915. Outer Mongolia kept Dariganga but lost Inner Mongolia and Hulunbuir [5]. Some New Barga bannermen and Chakhar soldiers were resettled in Mongolia as Damdinsüren’s subjects [5].

The two sides judged the result differently. Baabar sees real but naive victories: Russia let the war run only to press China before the three-way talks, and then stopped it [2]. Myagmarsambuu sees a major war that showed Beijing that Mongolia could not be taken by force and brought it to negotiate [3].

## Aftermath

The locally recruited soldiers were disbanded and sent home with their rifles, and for years they troubled western Inner Mongolia as *duli* (“independence”) bandits [5, 6]. Others became local militia [6]. Babujab refused to accept the treaty, and his band disturbed the frontier until 1917; Baabar writes that Khalkha troops under Magsarjav were sent against him [2, 5]. The war left Mongolia in debt to Russia: payments reached 332,000 gold rubles in 1916, more than a fifth of the central government’s spending [5].

## Open questions

- **The decision date.** The war ministry’s plan of 25 January [1, 2], the Interior Ministry’s order of 24 January [1], or Atwood’s 23 January [5].
- **Damdinsüren’s command.** Appointed overall commander on 1 February [1], or commander of the southern forces on 1 July [3].
- **The Yellow River commander.** Tachibana’s “Zutugeruto” and Baabar’s Sumiya may be the same man; neither source links them.
- **Jagchid’s commanders.** Whether Shirun *jiangjun* and Namsarai *bagatur* [4] appear under other names in the Mongolian lists [1, 2, 3].
- **Larson’s “Prince Na.”** Baabar’s Nasan-Arvijikh [2] and Atwood’s Nasun’arbijikhu [5] have similar names, but Atwood has the duke deserting to the Chinese, and no source identifies either with Larson’s prince.
- **How the eastern front ended.** Jagchid credits Chinese military pressure for the retreat from Dolonnor [4]; the other sources credit Russian pressure for the end of the war. The two may describe different stages.
- **Babujab’s rank.** A Kharchin duke and deputy minister [2] or a Monggoljin bandit [4, 5, 6].
