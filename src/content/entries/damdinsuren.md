Damdinsüren (1874–1920), known by his title Manlai Baatar, the “Forefront Hero,” was a Barga Mongol from Hulunbuir who became one of the chief generals of the Bogd Khan’s theocratic government in Mongolia [1]. He helped take Khowd for the new state in 1912 and in 1913 commanded the army sent into Inner Mongolia in the [Five Roads campaign](/entry/five-roads-campaign) [1, 2]. Like many Inner Mongols in Urga’s service he grew frustrated with the Khalkha nobility, and he died in a Chinese prison after Mongolia’s autonomy was revoked [1].

*A note on sources.* The entry rests on Christopher Atwood’s encyclopedia [1], with Tachibana Makoto’s archive-based study [2] for his 1913 appointment and two Mongolian accounts of the campaign [3, 4]. The fullest biography in English, Sh. Natsagdorj’s “Damdinsüren the Forefront Hero,” has not been consulted (see the open questions).

## Early life (1874–1911)

He was born the only surviving son of a *sum* captain in the Plain White Banner of the New Barga left flank, in today’s Xin Barag Left Banner. He worked as a clerk and enrolled as a militiaman at eighteen. In 1908, in Beijing to receive his lieutenant’s commission, he met the Khalkha prince Khangdadorji, one of the nobles who would lead Mongolia’s break with the Qing [1].

## In the service of the Bogd Khan (1912–1913)

In January 1912 the Hulunbuir bannermen drove the Chinese out of Hailar and Manzhouli and joined the new Mongolian state. Damdinsüren was made a duke, later a grand duke, and deputy foreign minister [1]. From 5 to 7 August 1912 he and the Khalkha duke Magsarjav led about 2,000 Mongolian soldiers in storming Khowd, whose Qing garrison had held out longer than any other, which rounded out the territory of the new state [1]. As deputy foreign minister he helped draft the Mongol–Tibetan treaty of March 1913 [1].

On 1 February 1913 the Bogd Khan appointed him overall commander of the army to be sent along five routes into Inner Mongolia [2]. Baabar also names him as overall commander [3]; Mongoltoli dates his appointment as commander of the southern forces to 1 July 1913 [4]. Atwood calls him supreme commander in the southeast [1]. Tachibana notes that most of the campaign’s commanders were men from Hulunbuir or Inner Mongolia who had come over to Urga, and cites the Mongolian historian Jamsran’s explanation: they knew the country, and they were fighting to free their own homelands [2]. The [Five Roads campaign](/entry/five-roads-campaign) entry follows the war itself.

## Later years and death (1914–1920)

The Kyakhta treaty of 1915 made Hulunbuir a “special region” of China, but some New Barga bannermen and Chakhar soldiers were resettled in Mongolia as Damdinsüren’s own subjects [1]. He proposed reforms against what he saw as the arrogance and mismanagement of the Khalkha nobles and lamas. After the disappointment of Kyakhta and the revocation of Mongolia’s autonomy in 1919 he drank heavily. The Chinese authorities in Urga arrested him on 10 September 1920, and he died in prison [1].

His name carried weight among the Barga for years afterwards. One of his followers, the New Barga herdsman’s son Ölzei, served in his army, rose to command an artillery unit, and in 1919 went with Damdinsüren’s aide-de-camp to investigate Ataman Semenov’s pan-Mongol movement at Dauriia Station [5].

## Open questions

- **His birth year.** 1874 in Atwood’s encyclopedia [1]; 1871 in Atwood’s *Young Mongols and Vigilantes* [5].
- **The date of his command.** 1 February 1913 (Tachibana, from the archives [2]) or 1 July 1913 (Mongoltoli [4]); the second may be a later or separate appointment to the southern forces.
- **The fuller biography.** Sh. Natsagdorj, “Damdinsüren the Forefront Hero,” in *Mongolian Heroes of the Twentieth Century*, trans. Urgunge Onon (New York: AMS Press, 1976), 77–104, and G. Myagmarsambuu’s volume on him in *Монгол Улсын цэрэг, цэргийн жанжид* (2012) are the next sources to get.
