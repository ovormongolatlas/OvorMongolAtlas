Sechin Jagchid (Chinese name Yu Baoheng, 于寶衡; also written Jagchid Sechen) was an Inner Mongolian historian who had first lived through the events he wrote about. The son of a Kharachin Mongol family close to [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub), he grew up in the prince’s autonomy movement, served in its governments, was elected to China’s 1947 National Assembly, and after the Communist victory taught in Taiwan and then for many years at Brigham Young University. His *The Last Mongol Prince* (1999) is the fullest biography of Demchugdongrub and the source this atlas uses most. He died in California in 2009 [2].

*A note on sources.* His own book’s “About the Author” note [1] and the biographical page of National Chengchi University in Taipei, where he taught [2], are the sources for his life. The university page draws on a chronology in his own oral history (2019). The two differ on his birth year and on his family tie to Wu Heling; both are given below.

## Family and education (to 1937)

The book’s note says he was born in 1914 in Inner Mongolia into one of its old aristocratic Mongol families [1]. The university page gives 29 March 1915 (the 14th day of the 2nd lunar month), in Beijing, with his family’s home in the Kharachin Right Banner of the Josotu League (modern Chifeng). His forebears held the hereditary office of banner adjutant (管旗章京) in Kharachin Right. His father, Lubsangchoijur, had been the administrative lama of the Fuhui Temple, and returned to lay life on the order of Prince [Gungsangnorbu](/entry/gungsangnorbu) to carry on the family line when his elder brother had no heir [2]. In his own book Jagchid describes his father as a Beiping Mongol elder who served Gungsangnorbu as his personal representative and helped launch the Mongol Delegation of the late 1920s [1].

He began at a family school, entered Beijing’s Huiwen mission primary school in 1926 and Huiwen Middle School in 1928, and in 1933 entered the political science department of Peking University. As a student he joined the Beiping Mongol students’ protest, led by [Wu Heling](/entry/wu-heling), against the Janggiya Khutugtu’s interference in the Mongol youth autonomy movement. His graduation thesis of 1937, on Inner Mongolian political questions over three hundred years, was praised by Tao Xisheng, Yao Congwu and Hu Shi, and he won a Boxer Indemnity scholarship to Britain that the outbreak of war with Japan cancelled [2].

His tie to Wu Heling is given two ways. In his book Wu married a niece of his father [1]; the university page calls Wu his elder sister’s husband (姊夫) [2]. The atlas does not choose between them.

## In the Mongol governments (1937–1949)

In 1937 he went to the Mongolian Congress at Hohhot as a delegate of the Mongol association in Beiping, and afterwards became an official in the government’s internal affairs office [2]. In 1938 he went to Japan as secretary and interpreter to Wu Heling, then speaker of the Mongol government’s council, and in his spare time studied at the graduate school of Waseda University [1, 2]. He found the working relationship with Wu difficult and resigned after about a year and a half [1].

Back in Inner Mongolia from 1939, he served in the [Mengjiang](/entry/mengjiang) government. The university page makes him secretary to the head of the Shilingol League and then chief of general affairs in the League office [2]. In his book he is secretary to Prince Sungjingwangchug, Minister of Civilian Affairs in the [Mongolian Allied Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-allied-autonomous-government) of 1939–41, which is the vantage point for much of his first-hand account of that government [1]. The two descriptions may cover different posts; the atlas does not merge them.

In April 1945, as head of general affairs in the Shilingol League office, he met [Khafungga](/entry/khafungga), the leading eastern Mongol official in Manchukuo, in secret during Khafungga’s visit to Mengjiang. They agreed that if the war turned, the eastern and western Mongols should act together. When Soviet troops approached in August, the Japanese special service ordered the teachers and pupils of the league’s middle school to arm and resist; Jagchid quietly sent the pupils home, which spared Shilingol a clash between Japanese and Mongols. He then joined the young men gathered at Demchugdongrub’s palace under the Soviet–Mongolian command, where the [Inner Mongolian Republican Provisional Government](/entry/inner-mongolian-republican-provisional-government) was formed in September. A Chinese general history of 2002 credits him and others with writing its declaration of independence and provisional constitution; his own memoir does not say so. These episodes come from the Chinese-language memoir of 1993 as Khökhbaatar retells it [4].

After the Japanese surrender he returned to Beiping. In 1947, nominated by the Shilingol League, he and his wife were both elected delegates to the National Assembly that drafted China’s constitution [2]. At the 1948 Assembly Wu Heling dismissed the demands of the [Mongolian Youth Alliance](/entry/mongolian-youth-alliance), Jagchid’s group, as “unrealistic” [1].

In 1948 he took his family to Taiwan and ran a “Mongol farm” at Pingtung [2]. In the summer of 1949 he went from Taiwan to Dingyuanying in Alashan to join Demchugdongrub’s last government [2], the [Mongolian Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-autonomous-government-alashan), where he drafted the Declaration of Mongolian Autonomy [1]. Before that government collapsed he flew back to Taiwan from Lanzhou on the last China National Aviation Corporation flight [2]. A US intelligence translation of the founding document of the [Mongolian Restoration Association](/entry/mongolian-restoration-association), the émigré body Wu Heling launched on Taiwan in March 1949, lists him, in Mongol name order as “Jagchitsechin,” among its founding members [3]. That the atlas’s most-used source took part in the ventures it records bears on his vantage point, not on the reliability of his scholarship.

## Scholar (1950–2009)

Recovering from typhoid in Taipei’s Mackay Hospital, he wrote two books, *Menggu zhi jinxi* (Mongolia Past and Present) and *Menggu yu Eluosi* (Mongolia and Russia), both published in 1955, and turned to scholarship [2]. From 1957 he taught Mongolian history part-time at National Chengchi University’s Graduate Institute of Frontier Administration. In 1968 he chaired the history department of the College of Chinese Culture, and in 1969 he became full professor and chair of Chengchi’s Department of Frontier Administration and head of its graduate institute; in 1970 he turned the department into the Department of Ethnology and Sociology. In 1972 Paul Hyer brought him to Brigham Young University [2], where he taught and did research for many years [1]. He also lectured at the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies and the University of Bonn, and in 1997 he was made a member of the Mongolian Academy of Sciences [2].

His work covered nomadic civilization, Mongol society, Mongol-Tibetan relations and religion, and translations of the Mongol chronicles, among them the *Secret History of the Mongols* (with Yao Congwu) and the *Altan Tobchi*. His Chinese-language memoir of Demchugdongrub and his times, 《我所知道的德王和當時的內蒙古》, appeared in two volumes in Tokyo in 1985 and 1993 [2]. He settled in San Jose, California, in 1996 and died on 19 July 2009 [2]. The book’s note of 1999 describes him as retired and living in California [1].

## As a source for this atlas

*The Last Mongol Prince* is cited in nearly every entry connected to Demchugdongrub and Mengjiang. Jagchid drew on his “own notes taken at that time,” on broadsides and newspaper clippings he had collected, and above all on his “own capacious memory” [1]. The book is unusually detailed and often corroborated by other sources in the atlas. It is also written by a participant from memory decades later, close to its subject and to the loyalties and quarrels of the movement, and it argues openly where Jagchid disputes other accounts, as with Demchugdongrub’s Chinese-published “Personal Narrative,” which he says was edited to put words in the prince’s mouth.

## Open questions

- **Birth.** 1914, in Inner Mongolia (the book [1]), or 29 March 1915, in Beijing (the university page [2]).
- **Wu Heling.** Married to his father’s niece [1] or to his elder sister [2].
- **The 1945 declaration.** Whether he helped write the Inner Mongolian declaration of independence (a 2002 Chinese general history, as cited by Khökhbaatar [4]); his memoir is silent.
- **His posts, 1939–45.** Secretary to Prince Sungjingwangchug [1], or secretary to the Shilingol League head and then chief of general affairs in the League office [2].
