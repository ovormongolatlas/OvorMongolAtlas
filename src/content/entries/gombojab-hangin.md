Gombojab Hangin (1921–1989) was a Chakhar Mongol teacher, editor and scholar. Educated in Japan, he taught in [Mengjiang](/entry/mengjiang), edited its leading Mongol literary journal, and in 1944 joined [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub)’s secretariat. In 1947 he co-founded the [Mongolian Youth Alliance](/entry/mongolian-youth-alliance), which sent him to the United States in 1948. There he worked on the Johns Hopkins Mongol Project, founded the Mongolia Society in 1962, compiled a major Mongolian–English dictionary, and became a leading figure in American Mongolian studies.

*A note on sources.* Much of this entry rests on one source, a 2022 memorial slideshow by Sanj Altan for the Mongol-American Cultural Association, marking the 33rd anniversary of Hangin’s death [1]. It is commemorative in tone, not peer-reviewed. Where it can be checked against Christopher Atwood’s study of Saichungga [2] or [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid)’s memoir [3], the entry says so; where a claim rests on the slideshow alone, it is noted.

## Family and early life

Hangin was born in 1921 into the Hangin family of the Plain White Banner (Taipus Left-Wing Herd, Chakhar), later reorganized as the Taipus Left Wing Banner. His father was Fullehengge (1897–1936) and his grandfather Samtanlhungrub, known by the honorific “Sa Amban” (1878–1932). His mother is given as Galindari (1899–2001). He married Sarangerel (b. 1923), and he was one of four siblings. From a young age he studied Chinese, Mongol and Manchu [1].

One of his siblings, the slideshow says, was the poet and essayist [Saichungga](/entry/saichungga) [1]. Both men are documented in the atlas, but their banners do not match: Saichungga’s entry, following Atwood, has him born in the Plain-Blue Banner of Chakhar [2]. Both banners belonged to the wider Chakhar grouping, so the difference may reflect separate registrations, half-siblings, or an error in one source.

## Japan and Mengjiang (1937–1945)

Hangin went to Japan in 1937 for preparatory schooling. In 1938, persuaded by [Wu Heling](/entry/wu-heling), he began forestry studies at Hokkaido University [1]. Only the slideshow records Wu Heling’s part.

He came back to Inner Mongolia in December 1941 and taught at the Mongolian Reconstruction school in [Kalgan](/entry/kalgan) and at the West Sünid girls’ school [1], in the home region of the [West Sünid ruling house](/entry/west-sunid-ruling-house). Atwood credits Saichungga with founding that school [2], so the atlas’s sources place both brothers at the same institution.

Hangin also edited *Manduqu Mongγol* (“Mongolia Rising”), the [Mongolian Autonomous State](/entry/mongolian-autonomous-state)’s main Mongol-language literary journal, published from Kalgan [1]. Atwood confirms it: Saichungga’s work appeared in the journal under Hangin’s editorship [2]. This is one of the few points where the slideshow can be checked against a scholarly source, and the two agree.

In 1944 he was appointed to what the slideshow calls the Office of the President’s Secretariat under Demchugdongrub [1]. The atlas’s other entries usually call Demchugdongrub’s office “chairman.”

## Leaving China (1945–1948)

Hangin stayed through the collapse of Mengjiang in 1945–46 [1]. In January 1947 he and Jagchid founded the Mongolian Youth Alliance in Nanjing. According to Sechin Jagchid, the alliance decided to send Hangin and Urgungge Onon to the United States to make contact with Owen Lattimore’s Mongol program at Johns Hopkins University and to press the Mongol cause with American officials [3]. He left for the United States in 1948 [1]. Jagchid writes as a participant, but his account is independent of the slideshow, and the two agree. The Mongolian Youth Alliance entry covers the alliance’s founding, members and work in full.

## In the United States (1948–1989)

Hangin was a research fellow on the Johns Hopkins Mongol Project from 1948 to 1953, took an MA at Columbia, and completed a PhD at Indiana University in 1970. In 1962 he founded the Mongolia Society. He became a major lexicographer of Mongolian, whose works include the *Modern Mongolian–English Dictionary* of 1986. He argued publicly for American diplomatic recognition of the Mongolian People’s Republic, including in a 1977 letter to the *New York Times* [1].

In 1987, with Tsorj Lama and Lopsang Khiyod, he co-founded the annual US Chinggis Khan Memorial Ceremony, an Inner Mongolian émigré commemoration that continues under the Chinggis Khaan Memorial Foundation and the Mongol-American Cultural Association [1]. The [Chinggis Khan worship / imagery](/entry/chinggis-khan-worship-imagery) entry treats the ceremony as a diaspora strand of the same symbol’s political history.

In 1989, the year he died, Hangin wrote a poem titled *Zangiy-a* (“The Knot”). Sanj Altan describes it as imagining Mongol identity as an invisible knot tightening across his life, from the age of ten to sixty, until he realizes the knot is the single word “Mongol,” given to him by his parents [1]. The atlas does not hold the poem’s text, and its practice with in-copyright literary work is to paraphrase, so only this description is given.

## Open questions

- **His mother’s dates.** The slideshow gives Galindari as 1899–2001 [1]. If correct, she lived to about 102 and outlived her son by twelve years. That is possible, but it is unconfirmed.
- **Saichungga.** Whether the two were full brothers, and which Chakhar banner the family belonged to (Plain White [1] or Plain-Blue [2]). Hangin published a study of Saichungga’s works in 1976 (*Tractata Altaica*, cited in Atwood’s *Encyclopedia of Mongolia*); the atlas has not seen whether it mentions kinship.
- **Wu Heling’s role.** Only the slideshow says Wu persuaded him to study forestry in 1938 [1].
