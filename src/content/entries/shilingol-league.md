Shilingol (Shiliin Gol, Xilingol) League was the grouping of ten Mongol banners that the Qing formed on the steppe of central Inner Mongolia, along the border of Khalkha (Outer Mongolia) [1, 2]. Atwood calls it traditionally the most isolated and nomadic part of Inner Mongolia [2]; Jagchid calls it the league with the best pastures [1]. Its prince [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub) of Sünid Right led the Inner Mongolian autonomy movement from 1933 and the Mongol governments under Japanese occupation [2].

*A note on sources.* The entry rests on Christopher Atwood’s encyclopedia [2] and [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid)’s history of Demchugdongrub [1], with Isabelle Charleux’s study for the banner maps [3]. Each banner has its own entry, with its maps as the cover.

## The ten banners

The league was made up of five tribes, each split into a Left and a Right Banner [1, 2]:

- the [Üjümüchin](/entry/ujumuchin) in the north-east,
- the [Khuuchid](/entry/khuuchid-banners),
- the [Abaga](/entry/abaga-banners) and [Abaganar](/entry/abaganar-banners), whose princes descended from Chinggis Khan’s brother Belgütei,
- the [Sünid](/entry/sunid-banners) in the dry west.

In the sixteenth century these banners belonged to the Chahar *tümen*. Many of their princes revolted against Ligdan Khan in 1627 and fled north to Khalkha, and in 1637, after the Qing had defeated Ligdan, they submitted and received land in today’s Shilingol [2]. Jagchid gives later dates for some tribes, from 1638 for Sünid Right to 1651 for the Khuuchid [1]. The league lay roughly between 110° and 120° east and 43° and 47° north, mostly steppe, well watered in the east and drier toward the Gobi in the west [1]. In all it had 113 *sumu*, companies of about 150 households [1].

## How the league worked

Under Qing practice the ten banners met in convention (*chiγulγan*) every three years on the banks of the Shilingol river, which gave the league its name. At first the emperor sent an envoy to supervise; in 1751 the court stopped sending one and had the ruling princes elect a head and deputy, and from 1787 Beijing appointed the most prestigious nobles as head (*daruγa*) and deputy head, usually for life [1]. A league head was a mediator among the banners, Jagchid writes, with far less power over them than a Chinese governor over his districts [1]. Most heads and deputies came from the princely house of Üjümüchin Right; Abaga and Sünid often combined to balance it [1].

## 1912–1958

In 1912–1913 the league declared for the Bogd Khan in Urga, after Urga’s troops seized Yangsang (see the [Five Roads campaign](/entry/five-roads-campaign)). Atwood writes that the banners were forced into submission to the Republic of China in 1915 and remained virtually untouched by the modern Inner Mongolian nationalist movement, Demchugdongrub apart [2]. Under the Japanese the Kwantung Army ran the league from its Special Service Office at the monastery of Beizimiao (see the [Abaga banners](/entry/abaga-banners)) [1].

After 1945, Atwood writes, Demchugdongrub’s modest schools were destroyed first by the Soviet-Mongolian invasion and its plunder, and then by a civil war between Communist Mongols under [Ulanhu](/entry/ulanhu) and anticommunist guerrillas led by the Buriat refugee Irinchindorji, whose last leaders were executed in 1952. The Khuuchid banners were merged into Üjümüchin after 1945, the new city of Xilinhot grew up at Beizimiao after 1952, and in 1958 Shilingol merged with Chahar League [2].

## The Berlin banner maps

From the early nineteenth century every banner had to send the Lifanyuan, the board that managed the Mongols, a written report on its territory illustrated by a map [3]. The Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin holds 182 such maps, collected by Walther Heissig and Hermann Consten and formerly kept in Marburg; twenty-two of them, catalogue numbers 807–828, are of Shilingol banners [3]. They are online in high resolution, and the library publishes them under a CC BY 4.0 licence.

Qing rules of 1805, 1864 and 1890 told banners how to draw these maps. Under the 1890 rules, meant for an atlas of the whole empire, a banner was treated as the equivalent of a province, and its map was to mark the boundary with ovoos, face north, use fixed units of distance and be drawn on a measuring grid [3]. Charleux finds that Inner Mongolian maps seldom followed the rules: they are more pictorial, pay less attention to boundaries, and rarely use the grid, except eight maps of Shilingol banners [3, n. 26]. Shilingol maps show many landmarks but few ovoos, usually one to five on boundary mountains [3, n. 87]. On the 1901 map of Üjümüchin Left (cat. 807), the banner’s eastern corner is drawn as a great forested mountain, Soyolji; it was in fact an imperial hunting ground outside the banner, ringed by twelve red circles marked *obo* and eight marked *qaraγul*, guard post [3, fig. 17a].

The anthropologist Caroline Humphrey reads these maps as statements made to the emperor more than as surveys [4]. They were drawn in the banner offices, usually by monks or former monks serving as clerks, from what herders reported, and sent up under the prince’s authority. The 1890 rules set a grid square of 100 *gazar* to one Chinese *cun* (3.2 cm), but mapmakers used other scales or left the grid out, and rarely said what a square stood for. The Mongol *gazar* was 500 double paces, which Shagdarsüren puts at 576 m. Orientation varied: the 1890 map of Üjümüchin Right (cat. 810) has south at the top. Some maps drew the banner as a ruled rectangle although no banner had that shape, and Chinese translations were pasted over the Mongol labels so the Lifanyuan could read them [4].

## Open questions

- **Reading the place names.** M. Haltod’s *Mongolische Ortsnamen aus mongolischen Manuskript-Karten*, vol. 1 (Wiesbaden, 1966), lists 13,644 Mongol place names from the Berlin maps, though Charleux warns of many mistakes in transcription and notes that he did not record the Chinese names [3, n. 28]. It is the key to placing the banner seats, temples and ovoos on the atlas map.
- **Which maps have a grid.** Charleux’s list of the eight gridded Shilingol maps (cats. 807, 809, 810, 812, 813, 814, 820, 822) [3, n. 26] does not match the online scans: cats. 810 and 822 show no grid, while cat. 824, not on the list, has one. Her catalogue numbers may follow a different numbering.
- **League heads.** When Sodnamrabdan left office and Rinchinwangdud took over is not given in the sources consulted.
