This entry rests on two kinds of source that see the government from opposite sides: two CIA field-intelligence reports of 1947, and the official gazetteer of the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region (below), whose account is set out after the reports’. Their limits should be stated plainly. They are contemporaneous but unevaluated informant reporting, anti-Communist in orientation, and they hedge the body's own name (one calls it the "Inner Mongolian Republican Temporary Government," the other the "Inner Mongolian Independent Republic Provisional Government"). One thing they are clear about is that this was a *western* Inner Mongolian body, distinct from the separate [East Mongolian People’s Autonomous Government](/entry/east-mongolian-peoples-autonomous-government) founded in the east in January 1946. The two should not be conflated; see the [Khafungga](/entry/khafungga) entry for the eastern movement.

At the Japanese surrender, Chahar and Shilingol officials, together with former officials of [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub)'s [Mengjiang](/entry/mengjiang) government, gathered at the Chahar Secondary Yellow Banner under a leader the report names Buyandalai (rendered "Buin-Dalai") and summoned banner delegates to an "Inner Mongolian Liberated Areas Meeting," forming an Inner Mongolian Liberated Areas Association (Source E). The stated purpose was to help the Allies expel the Japanese; the report judges the actual aim to have been to shift allegiance in time and gain recognition as anti-Japanese (Source E). Source E gives the steps in between. Not every banner sent delegates in time, but the meeting formed an Inner Mongolian Liberated Areas Association, with Outer Mongolians and Soviets watching the proceedings without taking part. At their insistence its headquarters moved to Sünid Right Banner, which the report calls “Prince TE’s former residence” and places at 42°40′ N, 112°53′ E. There a man the report calls Lamajabu (Sorinjabu), “vice-president of the Mongolian People’s Republic,” persuaded the leaders to go to Dzamyn-Üüd, on the Mongolian side of the frontier, where he told them the liberated-areas meeting was no longer needed: the Soviets, Outer Mongolians and Chinese had already liberated Inner Mongolia, and it was time to set up an Inner Mongolian government. The leaders agreed and were sent back after about a week. Because not every banner sent representatives to the next meeting either, it set up a “Temporary Government” until all could be represented, inaugurated on 9 September 1945 (Source E). The atlas has not identified Lamajabu, and the report’s coordinates for Dzamyn-Üüd (44°30′ N, 111°14′ E) lie about 100 km north-west of the present town of Zamyn-Üüd.

The constitution was short; the report characterizes its main principle as government “democratic and ultimately socialistic” in form, and notes that it said nothing about relations with China or any other state. The Mongolian People’s Republic and the USSR never recognised the government, but the report says they picked its leaders and advised them, controlling the Inner Mongolians as much as the Japanese had, though more indirectly, before stepping back to avoid international complications (Source E). The report also offers its own reading of motive: that the Inner Mongolians had been pro-Japanese only because they were anti-Chinese, and that any course likely to win autonomy was justified to them (Source E). That is the analyst’s interpretation, not a statement from the participants.

Its independence was brief. On 10 October 1945 a Soviet commander and Nie Rongzhen, of the Eighth Route Army's Jin-Cha-Ji headquarters, signed an agreement admitting Chinese Communist troops to Inner Mongolia. The Communists first "changed a few personnel," relieving Buyandalai, then sent about twenty men to take over the remainder, moved the headquarters to [Kalgan](/entry/kalgan), and dissolved the body into Ulanhu's (Yun Tse's) Inner Mongolian Autonomous Movement Association, with none of the original members receiving responsible positions (Source E).

A second report (Source F), reporting the government as having "died out" in September 1945, tracks its members' fates with careful analyst caveats at nearly every name. Buyandalai was rumored killed by Eighth Route Army soldiers on the way to Kalgan, but the report says this was disproven and places him in Nationalist custody, unable to say whether as protection or detention. Damurun, Jodbajab (with his family), and Mugdenbu were all captured by Soviet or Outer Mongolian forces and sent to Outer Mongolia, Mugdenbu later released and made commander of the Temurtu district Self-Defense troops. A Finance-Department member the report calls Jirgalang could not be located (Source F). The same report notes that Demchugdongrub's eldest son was captured with the family and sent alone to a middle school in Ulan Bator, a thread the [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub) entry develops.

## The Inner Mongolia gazetteer’s account

The government volume of the official gazetteer of the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region gives a fuller and in places different account (Gazetteer). It is a government-compiled history that calls the Japanese-sponsored regimes “puppet” governments throughout, and it is strongest on names, numbers and dates.

**Before the congress.** When the Mengjiang government collapsed, senior officials, members of the Mongol upper class and young Mongol intellectuals gathered at Taogaotu monastery (陶高图庙) in the south of Sünid Right Banner. Among the young men were core members of a Mongolian Youth Revolutionary Party founded in secret under the occupation, which favoured alliance with the Soviet Union and Mongolia. On 16 August 1945, at the request of Lobsang and Ivanov, who commanded the Soviet–Mongolian troops in Sünid Right Banner, the whole group moved into [Demchugdongrub’s palace](/entry/wangin-ortu). There thirteen of them formed an Inner Mongolian People’s Committee as a provisional authority. The thirteen included Jirgalang (Degulai), Mugdenbu, Buyandalai, Dugursurung and Delgerchogtu. The committee and the party formally asked Lobsang and Ivanov for the union of Inner and Outer Mongolia, and called the Shilingol, Chahar and Ulanchab leagues and the eastern leagues and banners to a congress in Sünid Right Banner to discuss it. When the Mongolian People’s Republic refused, the congress’s subject became Inner Mongolian independence instead, and a declaration of independence and a provisional constitution were drafted (Gazetteer).

**The congress.** The Inner Mongolian People’s Congress met for three days from 9 September 1945 at the Öndör (Wenduer) monastery in Sünid Right Banner. A 2017 report places the ruins of a monastery of that name about 1 km west of the palace; that it is the same monastery is likely but not stated by either source (see the palace entry). More than eighty people came: from the Shilingol banners, seven Chahar banners (all but Plain Blue and Taipusi Left) and the Dörben Keüked banner of Ulanchab. It adopted the Declaration of Inner Mongolian Independence and the Provisional Constitution of the Inner Mongolian People’s Republic, and elected a provisional government of 27 members, 11 of them a standing committee. The government had seven ministries: interior, foreign affairs, military affairs, economy, communications, general affairs and propaganda (Gazetteer).

**Its leaders.** Buyandalai, formerly president of the supreme court of the Mongolian Autonomous State and head of Bayantala League, was chairman. Damilingsurung, formerly commander of the Mongolian Army’s 7th Division and superintendent of the Chahar Right Wing Plain Yellow Banner, was vice-chairman and minister of military affairs. The other ministers and deputy ministers included Jirgalang, Mugdenbu, Tekhshibuyan (Wang Zongluo), Öljeiochir, Khorchinbilig, Bürensain and Golminse (Gazetteer).

**Its end.** A six-man delegation under Damilingsurung went to Ulaanbaatar to ask the Mongolian People’s Republic and the Soviet government to recognise the new republic, and was refused again. Meanwhile Tekhshibuyan, the minister of the interior, led a delegation to Zhangbei and made contact with the Chinese Communists and the Eighth Route Army. The Jin-Cha-Ji Central Bureau sent Yunze (Ulanhu) with Kuibi, Kerigeng and other cadres, together with young Mongol intellectuals then in Kalgan, and they reached Sünid Right Banner in early October. On Yunze’s proposal the provisional government was renamed the Mongolian Autonomous Government. Five Communists joined it, with Yunze as chairman and minister of military affairs, while Buyandalai kept his seat as a government member. The government was declared still provisional, pending a future congress. It moved to Zhangbei, and in late October most of its members followed Yunze to Kalgan to work in the Inner Mongolian autonomy movement, at which point the Mongolian Autonomous Government dissolved itself (Gazetteer).

## Where the accounts differ

- **The gathering.** The CIA report has the officials gathering at the Chahar Secondary Yellow Banner and forming an Inner Mongolian Liberated Areas Association (Source E). The gazetteer has them at Taogaotu monastery and then at Demchugdongrub’s palace, forming an Inner Mongolian People’s Committee.
- **Dzamyn-Üüd and the union request.** Source E has an MPR official summon the leaders to Dzamyn-Üüd and tell them to form an Inner Mongolian government. The gazetteer has the committee ask the Soviet–Mongolian commanders in Sünid Right Banner for union with Outer Mongolia and turn to independence only after the MPR refused. The two may describe the same refusal from different sides, but neither source says so.
- **The name.** The gazetteer’s name, Provisional Government of the Inner Mongolian People’s Republic (内蒙古人民共和国临时政府), is a third form beside the two CIA renderings.
- **Buyandalai’s fate.** The CIA report says the Communists relieved him (Source E). The gazetteer says he kept his seat in the reorganised government.
- **The end.** The CIA reports have the body dissolved after the Soviet–Communist agreement of 10 October, or say it “died out” in September (Sources E and F). The gazetteer has it renamed under Yunze in October and dissolving itself in late October. Wikipedia gives 6 November 1945 as its end, citing a copy of the same gazetteer text and other sources (Wikipedia).
- **Names.** The gazetteer’s Damilingsurung may be the “Damurun” whom Source F reports captured and sent to Outer Mongolia, and its Dugursurung may be Demchugdongrub’s eldest son, whom Source F reports sent to school in Ulan Bator. Neither identification is confirmed by a source here.

How exactly this western body related to the Eastern Mongolian Autonomous Government, and to Ulanhu's movement into which it was folded, is left open here. The sources describe the absorption but not a clean institutional lineage.
