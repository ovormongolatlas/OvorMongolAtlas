The Abaga (Abag) were one of the five Mongol tribes of Shilingol League, organized under the Qing as the Abaga Left and Abaga Right Banners [1, 2]. Their princes, like those of their neighbours the [Abaganar](/entry/abaganar-banners), descended from Belgütei, a brother of Chinggis Khan [1, 2]. In the twentieth century the Abaga Left prince Yangsang headed the league for three decades, and the Japanese named their Shilingol intelligence post after the tribe [1].

*A note on sources.* The entry rests on [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid)’s history of Demchugdongrub [1], Christopher Atwood’s encyclopedia [2], Tachibana Makoto’s archive-based study of Shilingol in 1912–1913 [3] and Isabelle Charleux’s study of the Berlin banner maps [4].

## Origins

Atwood writes that the Abaga and Abaganar were formerly the Ongni’ud, a camp district of the Chahar, whose princes revolted against Ligdan Khan in 1627 and fled north to Khalkha. After the Qing defeated Ligdan they submitted in 1637, received land in today’s Shilingol, and were renamed Abaga and Abaganar, “uncles,” for their descent from Chinggis Khan’s brother [2].

## Prince Yangsang and the league (1895–1924)

When the Sünid Right prince Namjilwangchug resigned as league head in 1895, the deputy head, Yangsang, prince of Abaga, took his place, and the Abaga and Sünid households grew closer still [1]. Jagchid writes that Abaga and Sünid often joined to balance the Üjümüchin Right Banner, whose princes supplied most league heads [1].

In 1912 Yangsang refused to pass on the Bogd Khan’s letters to the other banners. On 27 October troops from Urga came to his residence in Abaga Left Banner, exchanged fire with his men, seized him and his seals and took him to Urga; on 13 November he declared his allegiance and was kept on as league head under a new seal [3]. The [Five Roads campaign](/entry/five-roads-campaign) entry follows what came next.

When he retired in 1924 he tried to pass the league to the young [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub) of Sünid Right rather than to Sodnamrabdan of Üjümüchin; the [Üjümüchin banners](/entry/ujumuchin) entry describes the compromise [1]. By 1933 his son Budabala was the ruling jasag of Abaga Left, but Yangsang, Jagchid writes, still held the real power. He sympathized with Demchugdongrub at first and then turned away from him as too ambitious. Shonnudongrob, prince of Abaga Right, was one of the few Shilingol rulers who backed the autonomy movement from the start [1].

## Under the Japanese (to 1945)

The Kwantung Army set up a Special Service Office at Pandita Gegeen Keid, a well-known monastery in the centre of Shilingol known in Chinese as Beizimiao. The Japanese called it the Abaga Special Service Office, and it became their base for controlling the league [1]. After 1952 the city of Xilinhot grew up at the same monastery [2]. Both Abaga rulers, Budabala and Shonnudongrob, attended the Üjümüchin conference of April 1936 and the Second Mongolian Congress at Hohhot in October 1937 [1].

When the Soviet and Mongolian armies entered Shilingol in August 1945, Budabala, by then deputy head of the league, was at home in Abaga Left. The Soviets looted his house, and he and his father, then eighty, hid in the desert with their family. Abaga Right was occupied and Shonnudongrob was captured and tortured until the Mongolian People’s Revolutionary Army arrived. The Mongolian intelligence chief Duingkhorjab then gave Shonnudongrob and Urgungge, a senior official of Abaga Left, secret identity papers and told them to carry out tasks he would assign [1].

The 1901 Abaga Right map (cat. 820) is among the eight Shilingol maps that Charleux lists as drawn on a grid [4, n. 26], and Charleux reproduces from it the flat-topped “Boγda ula,” a mountain crowned with an ovoo [4, fig. 18a]. On the 1901 map of Abaga Left and Abaganar Left (cat. 816), twenty-one of about seventy mountains have an ovoo drawn on the summit [4, n. 121], and one is named Modon oboγa, “wooden ovoo” [4, fig. 4a]. The [Shilingol League](/entry/shilingol-league) entry describes the collection.

## Open questions

- **Yangsang’s dates.** Jagchid gives his age as eighty in 1945 [1]; his death is not recorded in the sources consulted.
- **Where the banner seats were.** Haltod’s *Mongolische Ortsnamen* (1966) lists the place names on these maps and would let them be placed on the atlas map.
