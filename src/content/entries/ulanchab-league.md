Ulanchab League (Mongolian Ulaγančab; also Ulaanchab, Ulanqab, and “Ulanjap” in CIA material) was a league of six banners in the west of Inner Mongolia: Dörben Keüked (Siziwang), Darhan, Mööminggan, and the three Urad banners, West (Front), East (Rear) and Middle [1]. Under [Mengjiang](/entry/mengjiang) it became a test of what a Mongol league could be once the Chinese provinces were abolished. Its princes held on to their hereditary rights, the regime kept them in office but never returned the farmland cut out of their banners, and between 1941 and 1944 the league fell apart [1].

*A note on sources.* Almost everything here comes from Hirokawa Saho’s 2024 study of the league [1], which rests on the minutes of the league’s 1940 conference, written in Mongolian, on Demchugdongrub’s autobiography, on [Sechin Jagchid](/entry/sechin-jagchid)’s Chinese memoir and on the league’s modern local histories. Hirokawa corrects the published lists of league heads against period sources; her list is followed here.

## Before Mengjiang

[Yondonwangchug](/entry/yondonwangchug), jasag of the Darhan Banner, headed the league through the 1920s and early 1930s and joined Demchugdongrub in the autonomy movement. In 1936 the vice head, Babudorji, jasag of the Urad Middle Banner, succeeded him [1].

Disputed successions unsettled two of the banners. In the Urad West Banner two nephews of the jasag who died in 1924 claimed the seat. With the backing of the Suiyuan provincial government, Sirabdorji took it in 1931; about 1935 the autonomy council at Bailingmiao deposed him in the league’s name, but he took back control and held it until his death in 1936. His widow, Qi Junfeng (Sebürma), then took the seat herself in 1937 and later sided with the Nationalists. In the Urad East Banner, when the jasag died in 1936, the autonomy council named his younger brother acting jasag, while the widow, Ba Yunying, joined the Nationalist resistance at Wuyuan after the Japanese took Baotou. Hirokawa reads these quarrels as contests between the province and the autonomy council for control of the banners [1].

## The new league (1937–1939)

After Japanese and Mongol troops took Suiyuan in October 1937 and Baotou in December, the [Mongolian Military Government](/entry/mongolian-military-government) was reorganised as the [Mongolian Allied League Autonomous Government](/entry/mongolian-allied-league-autonomous-government), with its capital at Hohhot. The new government abolished the provinces of Suiyuan and Chahar and organised their lands as four leagues, Chahar, Shilingol, Ulanchab and a new Bayantala League, with Hohhot and Baotou as separate cities. Bayantala took in the old Suiyuan counties, among them Wuchuan and Guyang, which had been farmed out of Ulanchab banner land, so the league’s old frontier was redrawn. That loss became the princes’ main grievance [1].

A league office was set up, and in August 1938 its organisation was fixed by decree. The league head answered to the head of the government’s administration and supervised the banners, the Chahar commandants and the county heads within the league. The four bureaus of general affairs, civil affairs, education and finance were replaced by a general affairs office over three bureaus, for civil affairs, livestock and security, and in September 1939 these became civil affairs, police and industry [1]. In September 1939 the league office had Babudorji as head, Sharabdorji, a Darhan noble, as vice head, Babudorji’s son Rinchinsengge in charge of civil affairs, and a Japanese councillor, Yamamoto Nobuchika (山本信親), beside them. Jagchid recalls that Babudorji did not come to the office and that Sharabdorji did all the work [1].

## The 1940 conference

In early May 1940 the league office held a three-day First Banner and League Administrative Conference. Hirokawa reads it as a way of winning the princes’ consent before the Mongol conference planned for Kalgan at the end of August. Babudorji stayed away and Sharabdorji chaired. The jasags of four banners came in person; the two Urad banners still in dispute sent officials, and the lama banner of the Siregetü Juu monastery sent its own representative. Prince Sungjingwangchug, the government’s minister of civil affairs, spoke for the government, with Jagchid as his Japanese-language secretary. Nearly everyone present was Mongol, and the proceedings were in Mongolian [1].

The banners’ demands were of two kinds. They wanted the jasag system kept as it had been under the Qing, and they wanted their land back: all seven banner delegations asked for the return of Wuchuan, Guyang and Anbei counties from Bayantala, and for the Urad banners’ rights over land inside Baotou, Wuyuan and Linhe counties. Individual banners complained of rents unpaid for decades by Chinese tenants who cited grants from the Republican government. Sungjingwangchug promised that the jasag banners would not be changed and accepted the Siregetü lama banner. On the counties he gave no answer, saying that the army and other departments had to be consulted [1]. Hirokawa concludes that the regime kept the princes’ offices while the land under them went on slipping away, which left the banners unsettled until 1945 [1].

## Collapse (1941–1945)

In the summer of 1941 Sharabdorji became league head and Rinchinsengge vice head. A run of crises followed [1]:

- **Mööminggan Banner.** In the winter of 1941 Asaka Shirō of the Japanese special service at Bailingmiao detained the jasag, Chimedrinchinkhorloo, on suspicion of contact with the Mongolian People’s Republic. On 2 June 1942 he shot himself with a pistol in custody. Many Mongols believed the Japanese had killed him, as Jagchid records, and the death split the banner. In March 1943 a party led by the jasag’s second wife, Erinchindalai, killed the meiren (banner official) they held to be the informer and fled to the Nationalist side [1].
- **Darhan Banner.** In 1942, on the orders of the jasag Tsesdbaljur, the banner’s soldiers made a night attack on the special service post at Bailingmiao, and in 1943 they seized a Japanese informer, in open conflict with Asaka. The Japanese adviser to Mööminggan Banner, Imamura (今村陽輝), later wrote that these incidents reached as far as the positions of the league head and vice head, and that Sharabdorji withdrew after the incident of spring 1943 [1].
- **The league head.** In June 1944 Rinchinsengge became league head and commander of the Mongol Army’s 6th Division, and his son Shongnodongrub a regimental commander in it. To celebrate he raised a new oboo, the Chogtu Oboo, south of Bailingmiao, with a great festival. The night after, when he and his father Babudorji had returned home, Fu Zuoyi’s troops raided the residence and carried both men and their families off to Shanba, in Nationalist territory, where they were held until the Japanese surrender [1]. Demchugdongrub’s autobiography does not mention it.

By the end of the war five of the league’s seven banners, the three Urad banners, Darhan and Mööminggan, were in disorder, and the league had no head [1].

## The Rinchinsengge of other entries

Hirokawa identifies Rinchinsengge (1898–1952) as the eldest son of Babudorji, jasag of the Urad Middle Banner from 1931, and the father of Shongnodongrub [1]. That settles an identification the atlas had left open. He is the Rinchinsengge whose unauthorised cutting of his queue in 1944 offended his father in the [Mongolian Autonomous State](/entry/mongolian-autonomous-state) entry, and the father of the “Shongno” of the [Mongolian Youth Alliance](/entry/mongolian-youth-alliance) entry.

## Open questions

- **The 1938 decree.** Published in August 1938, according to a period source Hirokawa follows; a Chinese general history gives July [1].
- **The Darhan raid.** 1942, with the seizure of an informer in 1943 [1]. An earlier version of this entry, built from a summary of Hirokawa, gave 1943 for the raid and described Chimedrinchinkhorloo’s death as a shooting by others; both are corrected here from the article itself.
