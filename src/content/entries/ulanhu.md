Ulanhu is the Mongol Communist who stood on the other side of the 1945 settlement from [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub). This atlas's sources agree on the outline of his career and differ sharply in how they weigh it. Atwood's *Encyclopedia* treats him as the Chinese Communists' “man in Inner Mongolia”; Jagchid, a partisan of Demchugdongrub, sees a clique built on Communist backing; Lattimore, from personal acquaintance, offers a short assessment of why the Party wanted him. The entry gives each in turn.

**Names.** The atlas uses Ulanhu. Atwood's headword is Ulanfu (also Ulanhu, Wulanfu, Ulaankhüü), and his Chinese name was Yun Ze (Yün Tse), with the childhood name Yun Qingchun (*Young Mongols and Vigilantes*, p. 279). Jagchid writes Ulanfu. Atwood says the name Wulanfu, which he took from October 1945, could be Chinese for Russian Ulianov (Lenin's original surname) or for Mongolian Ulaanhüü, “red son” (“Ulanfu”). Lattimore, speaking in 1983, states the Lenin echo as a fact and adds that a student in Peking could get away with it because the Chinese did not catch it (Lattimore, 1983 interview). Atwood leaves both readings open and this atlas does the same.

## Early life and training

Atwood gives his birth as 23 December 1906 in Tabucun village, to a declining Tümed Mongol farming family; he attended primary school in Guisui (modern [Hohhot](/entry/hohhot)) from 1919, entered the Mongolian and Tibetan School in Beijing in 1923, joined the Socialist Youth League and in September 1925 the Chinese Communist Party, and the next month entered Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow. He spoke Chinese and fluent Russian but only broken Mongolian. Unlike many friends, Atwood says, he joined no pan-Mongolist organization (“Ulanfu,” p. 570).

*Young Mongols and Vigilantes* supplies the village and school background from Ulanhu's own memoirs. The Yun family had once herded in the Tümed plains and settled long before; Tabucun was a village of mixed Mongol and Han households, renamed Tabusai in 1965 with Ulanhu's approval as a token of “nationalities unity” (p. 287). Atwood uses the memoirs as the only detailed account of a Tümed Mongol's early life, noting that the family could be classed upper-middle or even rich peasant by Communist class analysis yet felt poor beside the rich families' sons he met at school in Guisui (p. 279). He also notes that the memoirs were almost certainly ghost-written: the credited author is a Wulanfu Revolutionary Materials Compilation Office, and sources in Inner Mongolia say the party historian Hao Weimin wrote them from interviews and Ulanhu only signed off on the final draft. Atwood expects political distortions and says the memoirs can be used with corroboration (p. 287, note 16). The same book shows him at the Beijing school moving with the other Tümed students as a block, apart from the more numerous Kharachin students (p. 300), and records two accounts of a student demonstration there, his and Song Zhou's, that disagree on who led it; Atwood judges Song Zhou correct (p. 302, note 51).

On pan-Mongolism the sources are not quite the same story. Atwood's *Encyclopedia* says he joined no such organization. *Young Mongols and Vigilantes* says the example of the Mongolian revolution as presented by the speaker Sosordawa relieved “nagging doubts” about his ethnic self-worth that he recorded in his memoirs (p. 309), that he recalled being chosen by the Communist leaders to attend a congress of the People's Revolutionary Party of Inner Mongolia as a representative of the Beijing Mongol students (p. 455), and that when told to join that party he wondered why Inner Mongolia, which already had a Communist organization, needed another; Deng Zhongxia explained that the party center had decided on it as a mass organization (p. 463). Attending a congress and joining a party are different acts, so the two accounts need not conflict, but the atlas has not seen a source that settles Ulanhu's own party membership in the PRPIM. Jagchid lists him among the students the Comintern's involvement sent to Ulaanbaatar and Moscow for training, with his older brother Yun Run, Bai Haifeng and others (Jagchid, pp. 26 to 27).

## 1929 to 1945

In June 1929 the Comintern sent him back to Inner Mongolia with members of the Inner Mongolian People's Revolutionary Party to organize underground cells. From 1933 his task shifted to coordinating anti-Japanese sympathizers in military organizations, among them Demchugdongrub's own forces and the Tümed local militia. From May 1937 he served in the Ordos under the alias Yun Shiyu as a political instructor in a Mongol unit of Fu Zuoyi's army. He reported to Yan'an for the first time in 1938, was transferred there in March 1940, taught and sat on committees in the party's fledgling nationality apparatus until 1945, and in April 1945 was elected a candidate member of the Central Committee (Atwood, “Ulanfu”).

## 1945 to 1947: absorbing the autonomy movements

After the Japanese surrender, Atwood writes, Yun Ze was assigned to neutralize the pan-Mongolist movements in Inner Mongolia. In October 1945 he formed the Federation of Inner Mongolian Autonomy Movements as a front organization, and in April 1946 he pressured the East Mongolian autonomous government, then the most powerful in Inner Mongolia, to accept Communist leadership (“Ulanfu”). Atwood's entry on the Inner Mongolian Autonomous Region adds that he used diplomacy, threats and revolutionary appeals to push nationalist regimes and leftists such as [Khafungga](/entry/khafungga) toward merging with his front; that the new Inner Mongolian Autonomous Government was proclaimed on 1 May 1947 at Wang-un Süme (modern Ulanhot) with Ulanhu as prime minister and Khafungga as deputy; and that one-third of the delegates to the preceding congress voted against him as leader and were subsequently eliminated.

Jagchid, writing from Kalgan and Beiping, has Ulanhu's cadres arrive in Kalgan after the Communist forces did, trying hard to recruit the Mongol activists concentrated in Sunid and the Mongols in Beiping. He names the envoys Ulanhu sent (Shuwangchu, a former Beijing Mongolian and Tibetan Academy clerk, and Bai Wenhua, a classmate at the same school) and says some of those persuaded were pulled back by emissaries from the Nationalist side (Jagchid, pp. 344 to 345). He says the autonomy association was renamed the Inner Mongolian Autonomous Government with Ulanhu chairman, though eastern factions had more power until Ulanhu's group, as part of the Chinese Communist Party, gradually gained it; he describes Ulanhu and Khafungga as secretary and deputy secretary of the party's Inner Mongolia branch and says the leadership became “a clique led by Ulanfu” (Jagchid, pp. 370 to 372). That is a hostile Mongol-nationalist judgment and is attributed as such. For the groups that dissolved into Ulanhu's front, see the [Eastern Mongolian People's Autonomous Government](/entry/east-mongolian-peoples-autonomous-government) and the [Inner Mongolian Republican Provisional Government](/entry/inner-mongolian-republican-provisional-government) entries.

## Demchugdongrub and the west, 1949 to 1959

Jagchid records two contacts. In late 1949 Bai Haifeng and He Zhaolin, who represented the Western Inner Mongolian Government, met Peng Dehuai, Zhou Enlai and Ulanhu, whom Jagchid calls chairman of the Mongolian autonomous regime, to report Demchugdongrub's departure and their own surrender. Ulanhu told them to go back and win over Demchugdongrub and [Li Shouxin](/entry/li-shouxin), promising pardon, and gave them a letter for Demchugdongrub; by the time they returned to Dingyuanying he had already entered the Mongolian People's Republic (Jagchid, pp. 422 to 424). Jagchid also reports that in 1959 Ulanhu, as chairman of the autonomous region, announced that Demchugdongrub had been punished and sent to labor at the Fushan coal mines (Jagchid, pp. 442 to 445; see [Demchugdongrub](/entry/demchugdongrub) for the competing accounts of his fate).

## 1947 to 1988

From 1947 to 1966, Atwood writes, he held unprecedented power as chairman of the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, party committee secretary, and commander and political commissar of the military region, and he served in Beijing on committees for defense, nationality policy and North China party affairs. He was president of Beijing's Nationalities Institute and, from 1957, of the new Inner Mongolia University. Loyal to the Party and opposed to secession, he promoted use of the Mongolian language and delayed rural class struggle in Shilingol and Hulun Buir. Chinese and East Mongols both accused him of favoring Tümed Mongols, especially after the Hohhot area was added to Inner Mongolia in 1954. In May 1966 the North China Bureau attacked his “class reconciliation” and charged him with making Inner Mongolia an “independent kingdom.” He was exiled but covertly protected. He returned to prominence from 1979, was vice president of China from 1983, and died on 9 December 1988; a mausoleum outside Hohhot was dedicated in 1992 (Atwood, “Ulanfu”). His son Buhe was chairman of Inner Mongolia from 1982 to 1992.

In Atwood's entry on the “New Inner Mongolian People's Revolutionary Party” case, the purge that followed his dismissal fell on Mongols and on Han figures associated with his autonomy policy, with official accounts giving 22,900 killed and 170,000 injured or crippled (Atwood). In 1956 Ulanhu had to reassure former IMPRP members that they need not give further “confessions” (Atwood).

## Lattimore's assessment, 1983

In the 1983 filmed interview, [Owen Lattimore](/entry/owen-lattimore) described Ulanhu's Tümed origin among settled, farming Mongols and said he did not know Mongolian as a student and never became fluent. Lattimore's explanation for his success is that he was a desirable acquisition for the Chinese Communist Party, a minority member who could be shown as an enthusiastic party man, which he calls being “in between.” He says Ulanhu was eclipsed in the 1960s like many senior communists and later rehabilitated. This agrees with Atwood on the language and the Tümed origin and adds an opinion about motive that no other source here tests. A caption transcript of the interview garbles the name; the written summary gives it as “Ulaankh(f)uu.”
